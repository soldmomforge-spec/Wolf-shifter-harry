# Chapter Four: Full Stop

Jacob watched them come up the drive and tried to work out what was wrong with the picture.

He was standing at the far end of the glass wall with his arms folded and his hands jammed into his armpits to keep them still. Behind him the room had broken into noise the second Leah grabbed the guy's coat. Seth was still yelling. Emmett was laughing so hard Rosalie had walked away from him. Alice was on her toes beside Jasper, saying "I can't *see* any of it, I can't see *anything,* this is so *annoying*," over and over, like a kid locked out of a candy store. Nessie was on Bella's hip with one hand flat on the glass.

Out on the drive, under a black umbrella, Leah Clearwater had her hand tucked into the crook of a stranger's arm.

That was it. That was what was wrong. Jacob had known her his whole life. She'd babysat him once, according to his dad, when she was twelve and he was eight, and complained about the rate for a week. She'd been sixteen and loud and laughing at every Quileute Days he could remember, with her hair down her back and Sam's arm around her shoulders. Then she'd been eighteen, and Sam had walked into her parents' kitchen and looked at Emily, and after that she'd been something with its teeth showing for as long as Jacob could remember.

He'd been in her head for eighteen months. He knew what Leah Clearwater looked like from the inside. It looked like a fist.

The girl coming up the drive was walking slow. She was letting somebody else hold the umbrella. When the guy started humming, low and awful, she said "Don't" in a voice Jacob could hear through the glass, and it came out sounding like his dad telling his mom to quit it when she tickled him, a long time ago, before the car wreck.

It came out *fond.*

"Jake," said Seth, at his elbow, in cutoffs, dripping on Esme's floor. "Jake. Are you seeing this."

"I'm seeing it."

"She's *holding his arm.*"

"I see it, Seth."

"She doesn't hold *anybody's* arm. She doesn't hold *Mom's* arm. Last week Mom tried to fix her collar and she almost bit her." Seth's voice was climbing. "Who *is* he? Jake. Who *is* that?"

Jacob didn't answer. He didn't have one.

Across the room Jasper had gone very still at the glass. He had the look he got sometimes, like a man standing in a field listening to a sound under the ground.

"Jasper?" said Alice.

"I don't like it," Jasper said.

"Don't like what?"

"Her." He didn't take his eyes off the drive. "I've been feeling that girl every time she comes up those steps since August. It's always the same. Resentment. Grief. Something under it like a burn that won't heal." He frowned. "That's not what's coming off her now."

"What is?" said Rosalie.

Jasper was quiet for a second, like he was checking his work.

"Contentment," he said. "She's happy. She's walking up a driveway in the rain, and she's happy." He shook his head slowly. "It's like feeling the weather change inside a house."

"Dude," said Emmett, delighted. "Leah's *happy?*"

"I said I don't like it."

"Why not?"

"Because I've never felt it before," said Jasper, "and I don't know what she does with it."

---

They came in through the front door. The guy shook out the umbrella and leaned it against the frame, same as before. Leah came in under his arm, barefoot, with her shirt buttoned wrong and her wet hair stuck to her neck, and for a second she just stood there in the Cullens' hall while the whole room stared at her.

She didn't snap at anybody. That was the second thing. Leah walked into this house four days a week and she always came in already swinging — *what are you looking at, Blondie,* or *something died in here, oh wait,* or nothing, just her lip coming up off her teeth. Now she stood in the doorway with everybody looking at her and her face red to the hairline, and she said, "What?"

Not even mad. Just: *what.*

"*Leah,*" said Seth.

"Seth."

"Leah, you — he — you *kissed* — "

"I was there."

"In the *driveway!*"

"I know where I did it."

"In front of *everybody!*"

"That was sort of the point," said Leah, and Jacob watched Seth open and close his mouth like a fish on a dock.

The guy — Harry — had taken off his gloves. He folded them and put them in the pocket of the black coat. He looked over at Seth, mildly, the way you'd look at a puppy that's gotten its head stuck in a fence.

"She did it on purpose," he told Seth. "In case you thought it was an accident. I'd hate there to be any confusion."

"*Potter.*"

"What? He looked confused."

Leah hit him in the arm. Not hard. Jacob had seen Leah hit people. He'd *been* the people. When Leah hit you, you remembered it in your bones for a week. This was a little shove with the back of her knuckles, the kind of thing a girl did at a bonfire to a boy she liked, and the guy didn't move an inch and grinned at her, and she looked away and bit the inside of her cheek to keep from grinning back.

The room had gone quiet again.

Edward was at the piano with both hands flat on the closed lid. He was staring at Leah with an expression Jacob had seen on his face exactly once before, the afternoon he'd first heard Nessie think through Bella's skin. Like he was listening to something he hadn't been built to hear.

"Edward," said Bella, low. "What?"

"Nothing."

"*Edward.*"

"I can't get behind the door," Edward said quietly. "But I can hear the front of it. And she's — " He seemed to struggle for a word. "She's thinking about his ear."

"His *ear?*"

"That it's a stupid ear and she'd like to bite it." Edward's face did something complicated. "And that the suit's ridiculous. And that his heart's still going fast and she did that." He took his hands off the piano. "I've listened to Leah Clearwater think for nine months. I've never heard her think anything like that about anyone. I've never heard her think anything *nice.*"

"Bite his — " Emmett had his face in his hands again. "Oh, I can't. Rose. I *can't.*"

"I can hear you, you know," said Leah, without looking at the piano. "All of you. Every word."

"I know," said Edward. "I'm sorry."

"No, you're not."

"No," Edward admitted. "I'm mostly alarmed."

---

The guy didn't sit down. He hadn't sat down once since he walked in the first time, and Jacob was starting to understand why. Jacob used to do the same thing in Sam's kitchen, back when he was still in Sam's pack. You didn't sit down in another Alpha's house until you knew where the doors were.

"Jacob," Harry said. "I need a word."

Jacob's arms tightened across his chest. "So talk."

"Here's fine. I don't care who hears it." He looked around the room — Bella with Nessie, Rosalie halfway in front of them again, Carlisle on the stairs. "Saves me saying it twice."

"What are you going to do to her?"

Jacob hadn't meant to lead with that. It came out anyway.

The guy didn't blink. "Tomorrow at ten, I'm going to look at her, and ask her questions, and ask Dr. Cullen questions, and decide whether she's a danger to anybody outside this house. If she is, I'll decide what to do about it, and you'll hate whatever I decide. If she isn't, I'll write that down, and I'll go home."

"And if I don't let you near her?"

"Then I'll go near her anyway, and you'll be on the floor, and she'll have watched it." No change in his voice. Same as if he were reading Jacob the specials. "I'd rather that didn't happen. I'd think you'd rather it didn't, too."

Jacob felt the heat come up his spine. He felt Seth go tense beside him.

"You think you could put me on the floor."

"I know I could." The green eyes held his. "I'm not saying it to wind you up. You asked."

Somewhere behind Jacob, Emmett whistled low through his teeth.

"Jake," said Leah. She hadn't moved from where she stood in the hall. "He can."

Jacob looked at her. She looked back. Flat. Honest. The same way she'd looked at him the night he broke from Sam, when she told him *I always meant to leave.*

"How do you know?" he said.

"I watched him take apart a guy who'd been killing girls for a hundred years," Leah said. "It took him about four minutes. He was eating a chocolate bar for the first one."

Seth made a small noise.

Jacob held her eyes for a long second. Then he let his breath out through his nose, slow, and unclenched his hands one finger at a time.

"How old are you?" Harry said.

Jacob blinked. "What?"

"You. How old."

"Sixteen. Seventeen in January."

The guy stared at him. Then he looked at Leah. "*Sixteen?*"

"Told you," said Leah. "We grow fast."

"I said nineteen. In there. I called him son." Harry rubbed his face with one hand. "Christ. Sixteen. Sorry, Jacob. I'd not have talked to you like that if I'd known."

"You'd have talked to me different?"

"No," the guy said, after a second's honest thought. "Same words. I'd have felt worse about it."

Jacob had no idea what to do with that. Nobody had ever apologized to him that way. People apologized and took it back, or apologized and didn't mean it, or Sam apologized with his whole face like he was the one who'd been hit. This guy said he was sorry for one thing and not for the other and seemed to think that was a perfectly normal way to be sorry.

"What about her?" Jacob said, and jerked his chin at Leah.

"What about her?"

"What are you doing with her?"

"Going out with her, if she'll have me." Plain as a weather report. He didn't look at Leah when he said it. He looked at Jacob. "She's the best thing that's happened to me in about five years. I'd like her to know that. Now she does. So do all of you."

The room went so quiet Jacob could hear the rain on the roof three stories up.

He waited for Leah to go off. To tell the guy where to put it. To say something so cutting it left a mark on the furniture. He'd seen her do it to guys at the diner in Forks, to guys at bonfires, to a guy from the Makah rez who'd made the mistake of telling her she had a pretty smile. She'd made that guy cry. Jacob had been there.

Leah's face had gone dark red from the collar up. She opened her mouth.

"You're an *idiot,*" she said.

It came out wrong. It came out low and rough and soft at the edges, like somebody saying *thank you* in a language they didn't speak very well.

Seth sat down on the floor. Just sat down, right there in his wet cutoffs, in the middle of Esme's rug, like his legs had quit.

"I'm fine," he said, to nobody. "I'm good. I just need a minute."

---

"Second thing," Harry said, when Seth had his minute. "There's another lot. Yours aren't the only wolves round here. Who runs the other one?"

The room shifted. Jacob felt it. Every one of the Cullens knew the answer and none of them said it. They all looked at Leah.

"Sam," Jacob said.

The guy's eyes went to Leah. Something passed between them that Jacob couldn't read. A question, and an answer.

"*That* Sam," said Harry.

"That Sam," said Leah.

"Right." The guy's jaw moved once. "I need to see him tonight."

"Why?"

"Because on the fourth of December, a woman from Spokane called Karen Lindqvist was driving south on the 101 with her two kids in the back of a Dodge Caravan, about three miles past the Bogachiel bridge, at a quarter past six in the evening." He said it without looking at notes, the way Jacob's dad recited the old stories, every word where it had always been. "Something crossed the road in front of her the size of a moose. Gray, she said. Silver-gray, with a dark stripe down the back. She put the van in the ditch at forty-five miles an hour. Her son's fine. Her daughter's seven. Her daughter's arm's broken in two places."

Nobody said anything.

Jacob knew that wolf. Everybody in the room who'd ever stood near a patrol knew that wolf.

"She told the state trooper what she saw," Harry said. "The trooper wrote it down. A doctor at the hospital in Forks wrote it down too, and so did the nurse, and the little girl told everyone who'd listen, because she was seven and she'd seen a monster. Two days later three people from the American wizarding government came up from Seattle and went into all of their heads and took it out. Including the seven-year-old's." He paused. "It came to me. Because I signed a piece of paper in October saying anything about the wolves in this part of the world comes to me and nobody else. So it's sitting on my desk. Under the one about your lot's chief of police going out with twenty men and rifles last March, to hunt something that was eating hikers. And the one about the bow-hunter on the Calawah in June. And the trucker on the 110 in August." He looked at Jacob. "Eleven reports in eighteen months. All of them on my desk now. And the one with the seven-year-old in it is the one I'm going to see your Sam about."

"It was Paul," Seth said quietly, from the floor.

"I'd guessed it wasn't you," Harry said. "You smell like your sister. Whoever was on that road was a darker gray."

"Sam already chewed him out," Jacob said. "He was on probation for, like, a month."

"Probation." The guy looked at him. "A little girl's got a steel pin in her arm."

Jacob shut his mouth.

"What are you going to tell him?" said Leah. Quietly, from the hall.

The guy turned his head and looked at her. And for the first time since he'd come in the door he took a second before he answered, and Jacob, watching, understood that the words were already picked. What he was deciding was whether she should hear them first, in front of all these people, or later. And then he decided.

"That if his wolves don't stay off that road," Harry said, "there won't be any more wolves. Here, or anywhere your blood runs. Full stop."

Leah didn't move.

The room didn't move.

"You'd do that," said Jacob. His voice came out strange.

"I'd not have to. Geneva would. And they'd send me to do it, because I'm the one who took you out of the file." He said it to Leah, not to Jacob. "You asked me an hour ago what I'd do about the kid, and I didn't have an answer. I've an answer for this one. I'll tell your Sam exactly what I just told you, and then I'll tell him what he can do about it, and then it's up to him."

Leah held his eyes for a long time.

Jacob had seen people say things like that to Leah before. Sam, mostly. Hard true things, said in a level voice. He knew what her face did when it happened. It went white and shut like a door, and then she said something so vicious it took the skin off, and then she went out to the woods and phased and didn't come back for a day.

Leah's face didn't shut.

"Okay," she said.

"Okay?"

"You told me first." She folded her arms. "Kind of. In front of a bunch of leeches. But first." Her chin came up. "I'm coming."

"I'd hoped you would."

"I'm not coming to hold your hand."

"I'd not dream of it."

"Sam's going to hate you."

"Most people do, the first time."

"Not like Sam's going to hate you."

"Then I'll be very polite," said Harry, "and eat whatever I'm given."

Leah snorted. It came out through her nose, half a laugh, and she put her hand over her mouth like she'd been caught at something.

Across the room, Rosalie Hale turned around to look at Jasper.

"Is she still — "

"Yes," said Jasper, with real distaste. "Still."

---

Jacob called Sam from the phone in Esme's kitchen, with the lasagna steaming on the counter and Esme pretending to be busy with a dishtowel six feet away.

"Jake." Sam's voice was careful. It was always careful with Jacob now. "Quil came by an hour ago. Said something's going on at the Cullens'."

"Yeah."

"What is it? Another one of them? A nomad?"

Jacob looked through the doorway into the living room. The guy was standing by the glass wall talking to Carlisle in a low voice, with his hands in his coat pockets. Leah was perched on the very end of the arm of the white couch — the *couch* — two feet from him, swinging one bare foot, eating a grape out of the bowl on the coffee table that Esme kept for the pack. She'd never once touched that bowl. She'd said in August she'd rather starve.

"He's a guy," Jacob said. "From some kind of — government. Not ours. He's here about Nessie. He wants to see you tonight."

There was a long pause.

"About what?"

"Paul. The 101. The lady with the minivan."

A longer pause.

"How does he know about that?"

"He knows everything about everything," said Jacob. "He knew her kid's name. He knew how many places the arm's broken."

Sam didn't say anything for a while. Jacob could hear Emily in the background, the radio, a pan on the stove.

"Is he dangerous?"

Jacob thought about the guy saying *I know I could,* about putting Jacob on the floor, the same way you'd tell somebody the time. He thought about Leah saying *it took him about four minutes. He was eating a chocolate bar.*

"Yeah," Jacob said. "And Sam — Leah's with him."

"Leah's with him? Like, she brought him?"

"Like *with* him."

The silence on the other end of the line went on so long Jacob checked to see if the call had dropped.

"Eight o'clock," Sam said finally. "Emily's."

---

Charlie came at six on the dot, the way he always did.

Jacob heard the cruiser turn off the county road a mile and a half out, and felt the room go tight around him. Carlisle had already said, in his calm doctor's voice, that it would look stranger to call Charlie and cancel than to let him come, and the guy had thought about it and said, "Fine. Let him come. I'd like to meet him." Bella hadn't liked that at all. She'd liked it even less when Leah, from the arm of the couch, said "He's a good guy, Bella. Relax," and Bella had turned around and looked at Leah like Leah had grown a second head.

The cruiser came up the drive and parked. The door slammed. Charlie came up the steps in his rain jacket with his hat pulled down, stamping his boots on the mat, and Esme opened the door for him and took his jacket, and he came into the living room the way he always came in now — a little careful, eyes going everywhere and then deciding not to stop on anything.

Then they stopped.

On Leah. On the arm of the white couch. On the guy in the black suit standing next to her, and the fact that Leah's hand was resting on the guy's shoulder, easy, like it had been there a while and planned to stay.

Charlie's mustache moved.

"Leah," he said.

"Chief."

"Didn't know you were coming over." He looked at the guy. He looked at the guy's shoes. "Hi. Charlie Swan."

"Harry Potter." The guy put out his hand. Charlie shook it. Jacob saw Charlie's eyebrows go up slightly at whatever the grip was like. "I'm seeing Leah."

Charlie's eyebrows went the rest of the way up.

Jacob watched Charlie Swan look at Leah, then at her hand on the guy's shoulder, then at Leah's face, which had gone red again and stayed that way. Charlie had known Leah since she was in diapers too. He'd been on the boat with her dad more Saturdays than Jacob could count. He'd been at her dad's funeral, standing next to Sue with his hat in his hands. He'd seen her face there.

He looked like a man who'd walked into his own kitchen and found the furniture rearranged.

"Huh," said Charlie.

"I'm also here on business with Dr. Cullen," the guy said. "Family business. You've decided you don't need to know about any of it. That's a good decision, Chief. Probably the best one anybody in this house has made all year. I'd stick to it."

The room went silent. Every vampire in it stopped breathing at once, which most of them weren't doing anyway, but it was the kind of silence you could feel.

Charlie looked at the guy for a long moment. His face did the cop thing. Jacob had seen it at the station when Charlie was deciding whether a kid was lying about a broken mailbox.

"You a cop?" Charlie said.

"Near enough."

"Federal?"

"International."

Charlie thought about that.

"Is my granddaughter in some kind of trouble?" he said.

Bella made a sound, very small. Edward's hand closed on her shoulder.

"Possibly," the guy said. "I'll know tomorrow. If she is, it won't come from you. And it won't come from anybody you can do anything about." He held Charlie's eyes. "Keep not asking."

Jacob braced. Bella braced. Rosalie had actually taken half a step forward.

Charlie Swan stood in the middle of the Cullens' living room in his uniform shirt, with his hat still in his hand, and looked at the guy for maybe five seconds.

"Okay," Charlie said.

That was all. He nodded once. He turned around and held out his arms, and Nessie came out of Bella's arms and across the rug in a rush and climbed him like a tree, the way she always did, and he laughed and swung her up and kissed the top of her bronze head.

Jacob saw the guy watch it.

He watched every second of it. The kid's arms going around Charlie's neck. Her face pressing in under his jaw, the way it always did, nose against his collar where his pulse was. The way her eyes half closed. The guy's own face didn't change at all, but his head tipped a little to one side, and his nostrils moved once, and he looked at the kid's face pressed against Charlie's throat like a man reading something in very small print.

Jacob's stomach went cold. He didn't know why.

Then the guy looked away, at Leah, and said, "We should go. It's half seven."

"Already?" said Charlie, over Nessie's head. "You're not staying for dinner? Esme makes a lasagna — "

"I'm sure it's lovely," said the guy. "I don't eat here."

Charlie looked at him a second longer, the cop look again, adding it to whatever else he'd decided not to add up.

"Suit yourself," said Charlie, and turned back to Nessie.

---

They took the Rabbit.

There wasn't anything else to take. Jacob's VW was parked behind the Cullens' garage where it always was, held together with duct tape and prayer, and the guy looked at it for a while with his head tipped to one side.

"I can walk," he said.

"It's twenty miles."

"I've walked further."

"Get in the car, Potter," said Leah, and opened the back door.

He folded himself into the back seat of a 1986 Volkswagen Rabbit in a black suit and a black overcoat, with his knees up near his chin and his umbrella across his lap, and he didn't complain once. And Leah — Leah, who had called shotgun every single time she'd ever been in a car with Jacob since she was twelve, Leah who'd once punched Embry in the kidney over the front seat — got in the back beside him and pulled the door shut.

Seth got in front. He kept turning around in his seat to look.

"Eyes front," said Leah.

"I'm just — "

"*Eyes front,* Seth."

Seth turned around. Jacob started the car on the second try and pulled out down the long drive with the headlights swinging over the cedars, and in the rearview mirror he could see the two of them in the back. The guy was looking out the window at the dark. Leah was looking at the guy. She had her bare feet up on the hump in the middle of the floor and her shoulder against his arm, and every time the Rabbit hit a pothole her shoulder bumped against him and stayed.

At the bottom of the drive, where the gravel met the county road, the guy said, without turning his head, "You're staring at me."

"No, I'm not."

"I can feel it on my ear."

"Your ear's stupid."

"I've been told," he said, very gravely, "that you'd like to bite it."

There was a short silence in the back seat.

"*Edward,*" said Leah, in a voice of murder.

"He said it out loud. In the room. Several people heard."

"I'm going to kill him."

"He's already dead, technically."

"I'm going to kill him *more.*"

In the front seat, Seth had both hands over his mouth and was shaking.

Jacob drove. He kept his eyes on the road, mostly. The rain came down in the headlights. The wipers went back and forth. In the mirror, every few seconds, he saw Leah Clearwater lean her head back against the seat and look sideways at a man in a black coat, and smile at him in the dark where she thought nobody could see.

He'd been in her head for eighteen months. He'd felt every single thing she'd ever felt about Sam.

He'd never felt that.

He turned onto the 101 at the edge of town, heading for the La Push road. South of here, three miles past the Bogachiel bridge, there was still a long raw scar in the ditch along the shoulder where a Dodge Caravan had gone in at forty-five miles an hour. Nobody in the car said anything about it. Nobody had to.

---

Sam heard her laugh from the road.

He was at the kitchen window with his hands flat on the edge of the sink, the way he'd been standing for twenty minutes, watching rain run down the glass and the dark gap in the trees where the Rabbit's headlights would come. Emily was behind him at the stove. She'd made muffins, because Emily made muffins when she was frightened, and the kitchen was full of blueberry and cinnamon and the coffee she'd put on for a guest. Paul was at the table with his arms folded and his jaw set. Jared was leaning in the doorway to the living room, being quiet, which was what Jared did.

The Rabbit's engine came up the road, missing on one cylinder the way it had for a year. Doors slammed. Seth's voice, saying something about a puddle. And then, under it, clear as a bell across the yard, Leah Clearwater laughed.

Sam's hands tightened on the sink.

He hadn't heard that sound in two years. Not once. Not since the summer before everything, when she was eighteen and still wearing his ring and he'd thrown her off the end of the dock at First Beach in her clothes and she'd come up out of the water swearing and laughing at the same time. That laugh. Loud and from the belly, the laugh she'd had at sixteen behind the community center with the fireworks going off over the water. He'd heard her make every sound a person could make in the eighteen months since. He'd heard her snarl and spit and cry where she thought nobody was listening. He'd felt her grind her teeth on patrol until a molar cracked.

He hadn't heard her laugh.

"Sam," said Emily quietly, behind him.

"I'm okay."

She came up behind him and put her hand on the middle of his back, and he let himself lean into it for one second, and then the front door opened.

---

Jacob came in first, and Seth, both of them shaking water off like dogs. Then Leah.

She looked thinner. That was the first thing Sam saw. Thinner, and browner, and her hair was longer than he'd ever seen it, black and wet down her back, and she was barefoot on Emily's kitchen floor in a flannel shirt buttoned one hole off all the way down. She didn't look at him. She looked at the kitchen — the table, the muffins, the curtains Emily had sewn — like somebody coming back to a house they'd moved out of, checking what had changed.

Then she looked over her shoulder at the man behind her, and her whole face changed.

Sam had imprinted. He knew what it was to have the world tip over. He'd felt the floor of the Clearwaters' kitchen go out from under him when Emily walked in, and he'd felt Leah feel it, every night for a year and a half, in his own head. He'd have known that look on her face anywhere.

This was a different one. Smaller. Quieter. It was the look he remembered from before — from the dock, from the fireworks — a girl looking at a boy she'd decided about, all on her own, with nobody making her. It had been his once. He'd thought he'd spend the rest of his life knowing he'd broken it.

It was pointed at a stranger in a black suit, ducking his head under Emily's doorframe with a black umbrella in his hand.

The man straightened up in the kitchen and looked around it. He didn't smell like anything. Sam's nose went looking for him the way it went looking for everything, and found the rain, and the wet wool of the coat, and nothing under it, like a hole in the air. Every hair on Sam's arms stood up.

And on Leah, on her shirt and her hair, faint, something that wasn't Leah at all. Turned earth. Pine. Wet stone. A man's smell, all over her collar where somebody had held her head.

Sam put that away. He had no right to it. He'd had no right to it for two years.

"Sam Uley?" the man said.

"Yeah."

"Harry Potter." He didn't put out his hand. He looked at Sam the way Sam's grandfather used to look at a boat engine before he took it apart. "I'm with the International Confederation of Wizards. It's a sort of government. I'm here because a seven-year-old girl's got a steel pin in her arm, and one of yours put it there."

Paul came up out of his chair.

"*Paul,*" said Sam.

Paul stopped. He didn't sit.

The man looked at Paul. Just looked. Paul was six foot three and built like a door, and the man looked at him the way you'd look at a dog barking behind a fence.

"You'll be the one," the man said. "Darker gray. You were on the 101 on the fourth of December at a quarter past six."

"It was *dark,*" Paul said. "Nobody was supposed to — "

"There was a Dodge Caravan with its headlights on," said the man. "Do you want the plate number? I've got it."

Paul's mouth shut.

Emily stepped forward from the stove with the tray of muffins in both hands. She did it the way she did everything, steady and plain, the scars on the right side of her face pulled tight by the steam, her chin up.

"Mr. Potter," she said. "I'm Emily. There's coffee. And these. Please sit down."

The man turned his head and looked at her.

He looked at her face. At the three long marks from the corner of her eye down to the corner of her mouth, and the way they dragged the right side of her lip down, and the arm under her rolled-up sleeve. Sam watched him see it. Most people looked and then looked away fast, ashamed of looking. The man didn't. He looked for exactly as long as it took to see it properly, and then he looked her in the eyes.

"Thank you," he said. "I'm starving. I haven't eaten since Geneva."

He took a muffin off the tray. Then another one. He sat down at Emily's kitchen table, in Sam's chair, with his black coat still on, and ate the first muffin in three bites.

Leah reached over his shoulder, took the second one out of his hand, and bit it.

He let her. He didn't even look round. He picked up a third.

Sam had to look away. He looked at the window. At the rain. Because the last person Leah had stolen food from like that, without asking, without thinking about it, reaching across like it was hers by right, was him. French fries off his plate at the diner in Forks, every Friday night for two years. He'd used to order extra so there'd be enough.

"Sit down, Paul," Sam said. His voice came out rough. "Everybody sit down."

---

They sat. Jacob and Seth on the bench under the window. Jared still in the doorway. Paul at the end of the table, as far from the man as he could get. Emily poured coffee. Leah didn't sit. She leaned against the counter by the stove with her arms folded, close enough behind the man's chair that her hip touched his shoulder, chewing her stolen muffin, and watched Sam with no expression at all.

The man drank half his coffee. Then he put the mug down, and wiped his fingers on Emily's napkin, and started talking. He didn't warm up to it. There was no *let me explain* or *I don't want to cause trouble.* He just started at the beginning and went straight through.

"Eleven times in eighteen months," he said. "That's how many times one of your pack's been seen by somebody who's not supposed to see you. I've got every one of them. A trucker on the 110 in August. A bow-hunter up the Calawah in June. Three hikers on the Hoh in April who saw something they called a bear, the size of a pickup, black. That one's you, I think." He looked at Sam. Sam didn't answer. "Your chief of police, in March, with twenty men and deer rifles, out hunting whatever was killing hikers. The killing was the other sort, I know. Doesn't matter. He was hunting for you, and if he'd found you, he'd have shot at you, and if he'd hit you, he'd have had a body to explain." He picked up the coffee again. "And the Caravan. That's eleven."

"We don't — " Paul started.

"I'm not finished."

Paul stopped. Sam didn't think he'd ever seen Paul stop for anybody but himself.

"Everyone you scare goes home and tells somebody," the man said. "Most people don't believe them. Some do. The ones who do write things down, and put pictures on the internet, and every so often one of them's a seven-year-old who tells every nurse at Forks Community Hospital about the monster that made Mummy crash the car. And then three strangers from Seattle have to go into a seven-year-old's head and pull the monster out of it, and leave her with a story about a deer." He set the mug down. "I've seen it done to grown men. They go about for weeks afterwards feeling like they've lost something and can't remember what."

He let that sit.

"That's on you," he said to Paul. "Not on her. Not on the obliviators. You."

Paul's face had gone a dull brick red. The shaking had started in his hands. Sam could see it from across the table, the tremor going up his forearms.

"I didn't *mean* — "

"I know you didn't. Nobody means it." The man didn't raise his voice at all. "Doesn't matter."

Paul came up out of his chair so fast it went over backwards.

Sam was halfway up before he knew it — "*PAUL* — " — with the Alpha voice already climbing in his chest. But Paul was already going. Sam could see it in his face, feel it through the table, through the floor, the heat coming up off him like a stove door opening. Paul's shoulders were bunching. His spine was starting to bow. Emily was three feet away from him with the coffeepot in her hand.

Emily was *three feet away* —

Paul stopped.

He stopped mid-motion, halfway out of his crouch, with his arms flung out and his mouth open on a snarl. Like a film when somebody hits pause. Sam saw his eyes roll, wild, and saw the tremor in his body go on and on under his skin with nowhere to go, the change slamming into something and stopping dead.

The man at the table hadn't moved. He was still sitting in Sam's chair with his coffee in one hand. He hadn't even turned around.

"Not in this kitchen," he said quietly. "Not with her in it."

Nobody in the room breathed.

Sam stood there with the command half out of his mouth and looked at the man in the black coat, and then at Emily, three feet from a frozen wolf with the coffeepot in her hand. At the scars down her face.

And he understood, all at once and down to the floor of his stomach, exactly what the man had just done. And why. And that the man had looked at Emily's face five minutes ago, for exactly as long as it took to see it properly, and had known exactly how it happened.

Behind the man's chair, Leah had gone white.

Sam saw it. Saw her hands come up off the counter, saw her flinch back from Paul hanging there in the air like she'd been burned, and he didn't know why. Something in her face — some memory he wasn't allowed into anymore — and then the man turned his head. Just slightly. Just enough to see her.

"Sorry," he said to her. Low. Only to her. "Ten seconds. That's all."

Leah swallowed. She nodded once, stiffly.

The man stood up. He walked around the table to where Paul hung half-crouched in the middle of Emily's kitchen, put one hand flat on Paul's chest, and walked him backwards — Paul's bare feet sliding on the linoleum like a statue on a dolly — out through the open back door, down the two steps, and into the middle of the wet yard. Then he took his hand away and stepped back.

Paul exploded.

He went up into the rain in a spray of torn cotton and came down on four feet, a dark gray wolf the size of a horse with his hackles up and his teeth bare, snarling, ten feet from the man in the black coat. And the man stood there in Emily's yard in his ruined shoes, in the rain, without an umbrella, and looked at him.

"Go for a run," he said. "Come back when you can sit at a table."

Paul snarled.

"*Paul,*" said Sam from the doorway, and this time the Alpha was in it, all of it, the weight that came up from under the ground and bent your bones. "*Go.*"

Paul went. He wheeled and was gone into the trees with a crash of brush, and they listened to him go until the sound was swallowed by the rain.

---

The man came back in and sat down. He was wet now. He didn't seem to notice. He picked up his coffee, found it had gone cold, and drank it anyway.

Emily was still standing by the stove with the pot. She put it down very carefully on the burner. Then she looked at the man, and at Sam, and touched the side of her own face with two fingers, the way she did sometimes when she didn't know she was doing it.

"You knew," she said to the man. "When you looked at me."

"Yes."

Sam's chest closed up. "Emily — "

"No, Sam." She didn't take her eyes off the man. "I want to hear it."

The man looked at her. Then at Sam.

"One of you did that," he said. Not a question.

"Me," said Sam.

"In here?"

"Outside." The word scraped. "She was standing too close. I lost my temper. Over nothing. She said something and I — " He stopped. He didn't have the rest. He'd never had the rest.

The man nodded slowly.

"Then you know," he said, "better than anybody in this county, what one of you does to a person who's standing too close by accident." He held Sam's eyes. "Now make it a Caravan full of children, and a road with a speed limit of fifty-five, and a logging truck coming the other way."

Sam couldn't say anything.

"He's right," Emily said quietly.

"Em — "

"He's right, Sam." She came around the table and put her hand on Sam's shoulder, and he felt the scars on her palm against his collarbone, the way he'd felt them a thousand times. "You know he's right. You've known it since Paul came home that night."

Sam put his hand over hers and held on.

Across the kitchen, he saw Leah looking at them. At Emily's hand on his shoulder. At his hand over it. He waited for her face to do what it always did — the flinch, the shutter coming down, the lip lifting off her teeth. He'd watched her face do it a thousand times. He'd watched it from inside her head.

Leah looked at them for a long moment.

Then she looked away, at the man at the table, and put her hand on the back of his neck. Just rested it there. The man's shoulders came down half an inch.

Sam looked at that hand and felt something in his chest crack that he'd thought had broken all the way through a long time ago.

---

"Here's what happens," the man said.

He'd pulled a map out of his inside coat pocket. An ordinary gas-station road map of the Olympic Peninsula, folded wrong. He spread it out on Emily's table between the muffins and the coffee mugs and put his finger on it.

"The 101 runs from Port Angeles round to here." His finger traced the road west along Lake Crescent and then down through Forks and south. "The 110 comes off it to you. Somewhere along those two roads your wolves cross every night. I don't need to know where. I'm going to give you six places, and you're going to use those six, and nowhere else."

He took a pen out of his pocket — an ordinary blue ballpoint — and made six small X's on the map. Two on the 110. Four on the 101, north and south of town.

"I'll put wards on each of those tonight," he said. "Fifty yards of road. Anyone driving through while one of you's crossing will see a deer, or a dog, or a bit of mist, and forget it by the next bend. It won't hold up to a camera with a flash, so don't cross while somebody's stopped and taking pictures. It won't stop a car. So you still look both ways." He capped the pen. "You cross at those six places and you're safe from anybody seeing you. You cross anywhere else, day or night, and you're on your own."

"This is our land," said Jared, from the doorway. It was the first thing he'd said.

The man looked up at him. "The land's yours. I'd not dream of telling you where to run on it. The road's everybody's." He tapped the map. "This is the road."

"And if we don't?" said Sam.

The man looked at him for a long moment. Then he said it, the same way he'd said everything since he walked in: no wind-up, no softening, like a man reading the time off a clock.

"Then the next report doesn't come to me," he said. "It goes to Geneva. And Geneva won't send me to have a coffee. They'll send a committee. And the committee's answer to a breach that won't stop breaching is to stop the thing that's breaching." He folded his hands on the map. "There's magic that'll go into a family's blood and bind something shut. In the people alive now, and in everybody who comes after. Every boy and girl born on this reservation for the next five hundred years would grow up without ever once feeling what you've felt. No more wolves. Here, or anywhere your blood runs. Full stop."

The kitchen was dead silent.

"It's been done," the man said. "There were selkie families on the islands off the west of Scotland. Seal people. They kept getting seen. The Confederation went up in 1891 and bound it. There are no selkies in the Hebrides now. There are a lot of fishermen's families with stories about great-great-grandmothers who used to go down to the sea at night, and nobody believes the stories, and nobody ever goes down to the sea."

Seth had his hand over his mouth. Jacob was staring at the map.

"Who'd do it?" said Sam. His voice sounded far away. "Your committee?"

"Me," said the man.

Sam looked up.

"I'm the one who took you out of the file," the man said. "That makes you mine. If it goes to the committee, they'll send me to do it, because I'm the one who's met you and I'm the one who'll do it properly." He didn't look away. "And I would. I'd hate every second of it, and I'd do it properly, because the other option is them doing it badly, and badly with this kind of magic means children born wrong."

Behind him, Leah had gone very still with her hand on the back of his neck.

He knew she was there. He didn't turn around. He didn't take it back.

"Which is why," he said, "I'm sat in your kitchen eating your muffins and telling you, instead of writing a report. You've got six crossings. Use them."

---

Sam had been Alpha since he was nineteen. He'd been Alpha before there was anyone to be Alpha of, alone in the woods for two weeks with nobody to tell him what he was, and he'd stood up in front of the council and Billy Black and Old Quil and told them the stories were true. He'd given orders to boys who hated him and boys who loved him. He'd given orders to Leah. He'd given her orders he'd have cut his own arm off not to give, if there'd been any other way.

Nobody had ever talked to him like this. Not his grandfather. Not the elders. Not Billy.

He found, sitting in his own kitchen with Emily's hand on his shoulder and the rain coming down outside, that it came as a relief, almost. Like getting a splinter out. You didn't have to wonder what the man meant. He'd told you.

"Okay," Sam said.

The man nodded once.

"I'll tell them tonight," Sam said. "All of them. Paul too." He looked at the six X's on the map. "Nobody crosses anywhere else."

"Then that's done." The man stood up, folded the map, and held it out across the table. Sam took it. "I'll do the wards before morning. You won't see me do it."

Jacob, from the bench, said, "I'll tell mine too."

"I know you will."

The man turned to Emily. "Thank you for the muffins. They were very good." He paused. "I'm sorry about your face. Both for having to use it, and for what happened to it."

Emily looked at him for a long moment. Then, to Sam's astonishment, she smiled. The scarred side pulled down and the other side came up, the way it always did, crooked and real.

"I'm not," she said. "Not anymore. But thank you."

---

Leah stopped at the door.

The others had gone out ahead of her — Jacob and Seth to the Rabbit, the man down the steps into the yard to stand in the rain and wait for her without the umbrella, like the rain had stopped being something he noticed. Leah stood in Emily's doorway with her bare feet on the sill, and turned around, and looked at Sam.

He'd stood up when she turned. He didn't know why. He stood in the middle of his own kitchen with his hands at his sides.

"You're happy," Sam said.

It came out of him without permission. He wished he hadn't said it. He didn't know how to take it back.

Leah looked at him. Her face didn't do anything he recognized. No shutter. No teeth.

"Yeah," she said.

"Good." He meant it so much it hurt. "That's — good, Lee."

She nodded. She looked at Emily, behind him, by the stove. Something passed across her face then, fast, and he couldn't read it. Something older than hurt. Like somebody looking at a photograph of a house they used to live in.

"Your muffins are still too sweet," Leah said to Emily.

Emily laughed. It surprised her; Sam heard it surprise her.

"You always said that."

"I always meant it," said Leah, and went down the steps into the rain.

Sam stood in the doorway and watched her cross the yard. The man was waiting for her by the Rabbit. When she got to him she said something too low for even Sam to hear, and the man said something back, and she laughed again — that laugh, from the belly, the one from the dock — and hit him in the arm, and he caught her hand and held onto it, and she let him.

They got into the back of the Rabbit together, and Jacob started it on the third try, and the headlights swung across the yard and away down the road.

Emily came and stood beside him in the doorway. She didn't say anything. She put her arm through his and leaned her head on his shoulder.

"I thought I'd never get to see that again," Sam said. "Her, like that."

"I know."

"I thought I'd broken it."

"It mended," Emily said. "Somebody else got to see it mended. That's all."

They stood there for a long time with the rain coming down and the smell of muffins at their backs, and somewhere out in the dark, off toward the 101, Paul howled once, long and hurt and furious, and then was quiet.

Sue Clearwater was doing the crossword at the kitchen table when her son came through the back door like a bag of rocks falling down a staircase.

"*Mom.*"

"Shoes, Seth."

"Mom, Leah has a *boyfriend* — "

"I know."

" — and he's a *wizard* — "

Sue put down her pen.

"He's a what?"

"A *wizard,* Mom, like with a — he doesn't even have a wand, I didn't see a wand, but he stopped Paul from phasing in Emily's *kitchen,* he just *held* him there, like in the *air,* and then he walked him outside like a — and he told Sam if anybody crosses the 101 again there won't be any more wolves *ever* — "

"*Seth.*" Sue put both hands flat on the table. "Sit. Down."

Seth sat down. He was breathing like he'd run the whole way from Emily's, and maybe he had. Sue looked at her son for a long moment — fifteen and six foot something and still, sometimes, nine years old — and then at the kitchen door, where the rain was coming in sideways because he'd left it open.

And on the back steps, under the porch light, she saw her daughter.

Leah was standing on the second step with her face turned up to somebody taller, in the rain, barefoot, in that flannel shirt with the buttons all wrong. She was laughing. Not the short sharp thing she'd done for a year and a half, the bark through the nose that was more about being angry than anything funny. This was the other one. The one Sue had stopped expecting. Head back, shoulders shaking, one hand over her mouth like she was trying to keep it in and losing.

Sue sat at her kitchen table with the crossword under her hand and didn't move. She was afraid if she moved it would stop.

The last time she'd heard her daughter laugh like that, Harry had been standing on that same porch with his camera, telling Sam Uley exactly how deep the water was off First Beach.

---

They came in. Leah first, shaking her wet hair off her shoulders. Then a man in a black suit and a black overcoat who had to turn sideways to get through the kitchen door, with mud up to the ankles of a pair of very good shoes. He stopped on the mat and looked down at his feet.

"Sorry," he said. "I'll take these off."

He took them off. He stood on Sue's kitchen floor in black socks with a hole in the left heel and looked at her across the table, and Sue looked back.

Young. Younger than the clothes. Tall, black hair every which way, green eyes. A face that wasn't trying to do anything at her. Most boys who came into this kitchen tried to do something with their faces — be charming, or be good, or be invisible. Sam had tried to be good, at eighteen, so hard it had hurt to watch. This one stood there and let her look.

"Mrs. Clearwater," he said. "I'm Harry Potter. I'm going out with your daughter."

Sue didn't say anything.

She'd heard the first name. She'd felt it go through her, quick and sharp, the way you feel a step that isn't there in the dark. *Harry.*

"Mom," said Leah, from beside the stove. Very quietly. "I know."

"Leah told me about your husband," the man said. He didn't look away. "I'm sorry. I'll go by Potter in your house, if you'd rather. I'd understand."

Sue sat there for a long moment. The rain on the window. The old beige phone on the wall. The glass float on the windowsill over the sink, blue, the size of a basketball, that Harry had found on First Beach when he was ten.

"No," she said. "It's a good name. You'd better live up to it."

"I'll try."

"Sit down. You're dripping." She stood up and went to the stove, which was where she went when she didn't know where else to go. "Have you eaten?"

"Four of Emily's muffins."

"That's not eating. There's chowder." She was already getting the bowls down. "Sit."

He sat. In Seth's chair, which made Seth move, which Seth did without a word, staring. Sue put a bowl of chowder in front of the man and another in front of Leah and a third in front of Seth, because Seth was always hungry, and then she sat back down across from the man with her coffee and watched him eat.

He ate like her son. He ate like her husband. Head down, steady, all of it, and then he looked up and said, "That's the best thing I've eaten in this country," and she could tell he meant it, because he didn't smile when he said it, he just said it.

"Seth says you're a wizard," Sue said.

"Yes."

"Seth says you told Sam there'd be no more wolves."

"If his pack doesn't stay off the 101, yes." He put down his spoon. "I told him how to stay off it. I'll be doing the wards tonight. He's agreed. So has Jacob."

Sue looked at him.

She'd sat on the council in Harry's seat since the funeral. She'd sat across from Billy Black and Old Quil Ateara while they talked about the cold ones and the treaty and the boys, always the boys, around and around and around the thing for hours before anybody would say it. She'd learned to listen for what wasn't being said. She'd learned to say *hm.*

This one just said it.

"And Charlie?" Sue said.

Across the table, Leah went still with her spoon halfway to her mouth.

The man looked at Sue for a moment. Then at Leah. Then back.

"Charlie's in no trouble from me," he said. "As long as he goes on not asking. I met him tonight. I told him the same thing. He said okay." A pause. "I believed him."

"Is that a promise?"

"Yes."

Sue looked at him a while longer. Then she nodded, once, and drank her coffee.

"Hm," she said.

"*Mom,*" said Leah.

"I didn't say anything."

"You said *hm.*"

"I'm allowed."

The man looked from one of them to the other with his head tipped to one side, and his mouth went up at the corner, and Sue saw the tooth — long and white and sharp, a wolf's tooth, sitting in an ordinary man's mouth — and felt something in her chest go very quiet and very still.

She didn't ask. Twenty-two years married to a Clearwater, and two years with both her children turning into what they turned into, had taught her when not to ask. She'd ask Leah. Later. Or she wouldn't.

Under the table, Leah kicked the man in the shin. Sue saw it. She saw him not react at all, except to slide his foot over and rest it against Leah's, and she saw her daughter let it stay there, and go on eating chowder with her ears red.

Seth saw it too. He put his head down on the table.

He left at a quarter to eleven. He had six pieces of road to see to before morning, he said, and he'd be back at the Cullens' at ten, and Leah said she'd meet him there, and he said he knew she would.

At the back door, with his ruined shoes in his hand, he turned around.

"Thank you for the chowder, Mrs. Clearwater."

"Sue."

"Sue." He looked at Leah, standing by the stove. "Night, Clearwater."

"Night, Potter."

He went out into the rain in his socks, carrying his shoes, and Sue went to the window over the sink and watched him cross the yard. At the edge of the trees, where the porch light didn't reach, he stopped. He didn't put his shoes back on.

Then he wasn't there.

There was no sound. No crack, no rustle. One second there was a man standing at the edge of her yard in the rain, and the next there was only the rain, and the trees, and the dark.

Sue stood at the window with her hand on the glass float.

Behind her, Leah was washing the chowder bowls. Leah, who'd told Sue at sixteen that she was never washing another dish in this house as long as she lived. Standing at the sink in her bare feet, scrubbing a bowl, humming.

Sue didn't recognize the song at first. Then she did. Low, off-key, terrible.

Creedence.

She stood there with her hand on her husband's glass float and her back to her daughter and didn't turn around, and didn't say *hm,* and didn't say anything at all, because if she'd turned around Leah would have seen her face.

At four minutes to ten on Christmas Eve morning, Rosalie Hale stood at the glass wall with her arms folded and watched Leah Clearwater walk up the drive carrying two cups of coffee.

Two. In cardboard cups from the diner in Forks, with lids. One in each hand.

Beside her, Leah was walking with the wizard. He'd changed his shoes. He was in boots this morning, heavy brown ones, under the same long black coat, and he had no umbrella, and the rain had eased to a fine gray mist that beaded on his hair and on hers. She handed him one of the cups without looking at him. He took it without looking at her. They came up the drive side by side, drinking coffee, not talking, their shoulders an inch apart, like two people who'd been walking up driveways together for years.

"She bought him coffee," Rosalie said.

"I see that," said Emmett.

"She has never bought anybody anything. She's never brought so much as a stick of gum into this house. Esme leaves her coffee on the porch every day and she drinks it like she's doing us a favor."

"Babe."

"She bought him *coffee.*"

"It's Christmas," said Emmett. "People get weird at Christmas."

Behind them the house smelled of pine. Esme had put up the tree on the first of December, a fourteen-foot Douglas fir from somewhere up the Sol Duc, and Alice had decorated it, twice, because the first time she'd been unable to see whether Renesmee would like it and had panicked. There were presents under it already. Most of them were for Renesmee. Rosalie had bought eleven of them herself.

She'd been awake all night, if that was the word for what she did. They all had. Nobody had gone to the cottage. Bella had sat on the couch until dawn with Renesmee asleep against her chest, not moving, staring at the door. Jacob had slept on the floor at Bella's feet, and twitched, and growled in his sleep. Rosalie had stood at the glass and watched the drive.

*Appropriate action.*

She'd killed five men in her life. She'd done it in her wedding dress, with her own two hands, slowly, and she'd never drunk a drop from any of them because she hadn't wanted any part of them inside her. The wizard knew that. It was in his file. She'd stood in this room yesterday afternoon with every muscle locked, waiting for him to say *Rochester* out loud in front of all of them, the way he'd said *Tennessee, a bear* to Emmett, and *Southwark* to Carlisle.

He'd said *Miss Hale,* and nodded, and moved on.

She hadn't decided yet what she thought about that. She'd thought about it most of the night.

The front door opened. Leah came in first. She stopped in the hall and looked at Rosalie at the glass.

"Morning, Rosalie," Leah said.

Rosalie stared at her.

*Rosalie.* Not *Blondie.* Not *Barbie,* or *the blonde one,* or *hey, leech,* or nothing at all, which was what Leah said to her most days, nothing, with her lip lifting off her teeth. *Morning, Rosalie,* in an ordinary voice, like a person.

"Good morning," Rosalie heard herself say. It came out stiff.

Leah shrugged and went to lean against the wall by the kitchen door with her coffee. The wizard came in behind her, set his cup down on the hall table, pulled off his coat, and hung it on the hook where Charlie hung his. Under it he was in a dark sweater with the sleeves pushed up. No suit. No tie.

"Dr. Cullen," he said. "Mrs. Cullen. Let's do this in the kitchen, if that's all right. I'd like a table."

They went into the kitchen. All of them. Nobody suggested otherwise.

Bella sat at the end of the long table with Renesmee on her lap. Edward stood behind her with both hands on her shoulders. Jacob stood behind Edward, which was a sight Rosalie had never expected to see in her life. Carlisle sat on Bella's right with a thick folder of notes. Esme stood at the counter. Jasper and Alice took the corner by the window. Emmett and Rosalie stood together by the refrigerator they kept full for the dogs, and Leah leaned in the doorway, and the wizard pulled out the chair opposite Bella and sat down.

He looked at Renesmee for a long moment across the table. She looked back. She had her thumb in her mouth, which she hadn't done in weeks.

"Hello," he said to her.

"Hello," said Renesmee, around the thumb.

"I'm Harry. I'm going to ask you some questions. Some of them are going to be about things your mum won't like me asking. You can answer or not. If you don't want to answer, say so. Don't make something up. All right?"

Renesmee took her thumb out of her mouth. "All right."

"Good." He turned to Carlisle. "Notes."

Carlisle slid the folder across. The wizard opened it and read. He read fast, turning pages with one finger, and the kitchen was so quiet Rosalie could hear the paper. Height. Weight. Carlisle's eyebrows went up. "It's on page — "

She held her hand out.

Then her face changed. It lit up, all at once, the way it did when Jacob came through the door, and the wizard's eyes came open and went wide, and then — Rosalie saw it happen, saw it go across his face and couldn't stop it — he laughed. Out loud, in the middle of the Cullens' kitchen, surprised out of him.

"That was from the window," he said.

Renesmee nodded happily. "Leah was happy."

In the doorway, Leah choked on her coffee.

"Leah's never happy," Renesmee explained to the table, very seriously, taking her hand back. "I've watched. She's always cross. Even when she's eating. But in the rain, with you, she was happy. It was a different color." She looked at Leah in the doorway. "You should do that more."

Leah stood in the doorway with coffee down the front of her shirt and her face the color of a brick.

Nobody said anything. Emmett had both hands over his mouth. Jasper, in the corner, closed his eyes like a man with a migraine.

"She's three months old," Leah said to the room, in a strangled voice, "and she's *spying* on me."

"She's very observant," said the wizard. He was still grinning. "I'll put it in the report."

---

Then the grin went away.

He folded his hands on the table and looked at Renesmee, and his face went back to what it had been yesterday: level, steady, giving nothing.

"Do you drink blood?" he said.

Bella's arms went rigid. Renesmee answered anyway.

"Yes."

"Do you like it better than food?"

"Yes. Food tastes like dirt. Grandma makes me eat eggs."

"Have you ever bitten anyone?"

Renesmee thought. "Mommy," she said. "When I was born. I didn't know."

"Anyone since?"

"No."

"Has anyone ever been hurt because of you?"

Renesmee looked up at Bella. Bella's jaw was clenched so hard Rosalie could hear it. Then the child looked back at the wizard.

"Mommy died," she said. "A little. Because of me. Grandpa Carlisle says it wasn't my fault. Jacob says it wasn't my fault." Her voice was very small. "Was it?"

The wizard looked at her for a long moment.

"No," he said. "Being born was your mum and dad's decision, and it nearly killed her, and that's theirs to carry." Bella flinched as if he'd slapped her. He didn't look at Bella. "You're not to carry it. You hear me?"

Renesmee nodded slowly.

"Last question," he said. "Do people smell good to you?"

Rosalie felt every vampire in the kitchen go still.

They'd never asked her that. Not one of them. They'd watched her. They'd watched her face when Charlie came in the door. They'd seen her nose go into his collar every Sunday, every single time, and her eyes half close. Rosalie had watched it fifty times and told herself it was love. And none of them, not Carlisle with his folder or Edward with his gift or Bella who'd have died for her, had ever once asked her the question out loud, because they were all afraid of the answer.

Renesmee looked at the man across the table. She didn't look at her mother. She didn't look at Jacob.

"Grandpa smells best," she said.

Nobody moved.

"Do you want to bite him?"

"*Sometimes,*" Renesmee said. Very small. Very honest. "When he picks me up. He smells like — " She stopped. Her face crumpled for a second and then smoothed. "Sometimes."

Bella made a sound Rosalie had never heard her make. Jacob's hand had gone over his mouth.

The wizard didn't move. He didn't look away from the child.

"You don't, though," he said.

"No."

"Why not?"

Renesmee thought about it for a long time. Long enough that Rosalie found she'd stopped breathing, which she didn't need to do, which she did anyway.

"Because then he'd be scared of me," Renesmee said. "And he'd stop coming."

The wizard sat back in his chair.

He looked at the child across the table for a long, long moment. Then he let out a breath, slow, through his nose, and something in his shoulders came down that had been up since he walked in yesterday afternoon.

"Thank you," he said to Renesmee. "That's the most honest thing anybody's said to me in this house. Including me."

He stood up.

"I'll tell you what I've decided at three o'clock," he said to Carlisle, to Bella, to all of them. "I need to tell one person first. I said I would."

He looked at the doorway.

Rosalie followed his eyes, and so did every vampire in the kitchen, and so did Jacob, to Leah Clearwater, standing there with coffee all down her shirt and her arms wrapped around herself, looking back at him.

Rosalie waited to feel what she ought to feel about that. The insult of it. The dog finding out first, before Bella, before Carlisle, before any of them, what was going to happen to their child. She waited for the anger to come up the way it always came up when Leah Clearwater walked into this house.

It didn't come.

She found she was thinking instead about the word *Rochester,* and about a man sitting across a table from a three-month-old and asking her the one question none of them had dared to, and taking the answer without flinching, and saying *thank you.* About a man who'd looked at her yesterday and said her name and nothing else.

She'd spent seventy years waiting for someone to tell her the truth to her face. Mostly they hadn't. Mostly they'd been afraid.

"Go on, then," Rosalie said to Leah.

Leah blinked at her.

"Go on," Rosalie said again. Cold, because she didn't know how else to say it. "He's waiting for you. Don't make him wait."

Leah looked at her for a long second. Then, unbelievably, she nodded. Once. The way you'd nod to somebody across a fence.

She went out through the back door, and the wizard picked his coat up off Charlie's hook and followed her, and the door swung shut behind them.

In the kitchen nobody said anything for a long time.

Then Renesmee, on Bella's lap, said in a small clear voice, "I like him."

"Yeah," said Emmett faintly. "Yeah, kid. Weirdly. Me too."

---

She took him down to the river.

Not far. A couple of hundred yards behind the house, through the ferns, to the bank where the gravel ran out into the water and the alders leaned over it, bare and gray. The rain had stopped. Mist was coming up off the river in long slow sheets. Across the water, the far bank was a dark smudge of cedar.

It was where Seth had sat. She hadn't meant to bring him here. Her feet had done it.

She stood on the gravel with her arms wrapped around herself and her shirt still sticky with coffee, and didn't look at him.

"Go on," she said.

He stood beside her with his hands in his coat pockets, looking at the water.

"She stays," Harry said.

Leah's knees went loose. She locked them.

"Alive. With them. Here, for now." He said it to the river, plainly, the way he'd said everything. "I'm going to register her with the Confederation under my name, same as I did with your lot. Anybody who wants to know about her has to come through me."

"That's it?"

"There's conditions. I'll give them the list at three. You can hear it now." He held up one finger. "Charlie keeps what he knows, as long as he never asks for more. If he asks, I'll take it, and I'll do it myself, and he'll never feel it." A second finger. "She never drinks from a person. Not a sip, not a willing one, not her grandfather if he offers. That one's the one that matters. If she breaks it, I'll be back, and I'll have stopped talking." Third. "They leave Forks before she looks old enough for school. Two years at the outside. Somewhere bigger, with fewer people who knew Bella Swan." Fourth. "I see her every three months. For as long as I'm alive. I'll be the one who decides if she's still safe, and I'll be honest with them when she isn't." He put his hand back in his pocket. "And your Sam keeps his wolves off the road. That one's on your side of things. It's the same breach, and it's going in the same report."

Leah breathed out. It shook.

"What changed your mind?" she said. "Yesterday you looked at her like she was a bomb."

"She was, yesterday. I'd only got the file." He was quiet for a moment. "She told the truth. About her grandfather. Three months old, and she looked at me across a table with her mum's arms round her and said *sometimes,* when every person in that kitchen wanted her to say *never.* And then she told me why she doesn't." He shook his head slowly. "I've arrested grown wizards with a hundred years on her who couldn't do that. Who'd never once in their lives not done a thing just because it'd frighten someone they loved."

"She might still do it. One day."

"She might," he said. "I could be wrong about her. I've been wrong before. That's why it's every three months, and why it's my name on the paper." He looked at her for the first time since they'd come down to the water. "If I'm wrong, it's my head in Geneva. I can live with that. I'd rather be wrong this way than the other."

The Italians, Leah thought. He hadn't said anything about the Italians. The ones in the cloaks.

"What about the leeches in Italy?" she said. "The ones who came last spring."

"They'll hear about her. They always hear." His jaw moved. "When they come looking, they'll find my name on her paperwork. They're very old and very careful, going by the file, and careful people don't pick a fight with the Confederation over one little girl in Washington." He looked back at the water. "If they do, they'll be picking it with me."

---

They stood there for a while. The mist came up off the water. Somewhere downstream a heron took off with a sound like somebody shaking out a rug.

"Why'd you tell me first?" Leah said.

"I said I would."

"Before her own mother. Before Carlisle. Before any of them."

"Yes."

"They'll hate that."

"Rosalie told you to go," he said. "I heard her. I don't think they hate it as much as they'd like to." He turned toward her a little. "And it's not the only reason."

She waited.

He didn't wind up to it. He didn't take a breath first or look away or put his hands anywhere different. He stood on the gravel with the mist coming up behind him and looked at her and said it the way he'd said *she stays.*

"I'm in love with you," Harry said.

The river went on going by.

"I've known since the plateau," he said. "Probably since you took my fork. I'd like you to know it. You don't have to do anything about it. You don't have to say anything back. You've had a lot of people decide things about you without bothering to tell you. I'm telling you."

Leah stood on the bank where her brother had sat every night waiting for her, and felt her face go hot, and her eyes sting, and her heart go hard and fast under her ribs, and checked. The way she always checked. For the floor to go out from under her. For gravity to slide, and everything else to go light, and somebody else's decision to land on her like a car.

The floor was gravel. It stayed gravel.

Nothing had happened to her. Somebody had told her something. That was all. He'd told her and then stood there and waited, with his hands in his pockets, like he'd have stood there all day if she needed all day.

"You can't just *say* that," she said. It came out cracked.

"I just did."

"We've been on four dates."

"Five. And about forty hours on a mirror. One of them from a bath."

"That's — " She stopped. She wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, furious. "That's *insane.* You know that's insane."

"Probably," he said. "Doesn't make it less true."

She turned around and looked at the river so she wouldn't have to look at him. Her hands were shaking. She stuffed them in her armpits.

For a year and a half everybody had known what Leah felt before she did. They'd felt it in her head. They'd told her about it. *Leah, you need to let go. Leah, you're bitter. Leah, you're scaring the younger ones.* And nobody, not one single person, had ever stood in front of her and said what *they* felt and then just — left it there. For her to pick up or not. Like a coffee on a porch rail.

"Okay," she said.

"Okay?"

"I heard you." She didn't turn around. "I'm thinking about it."

"Fair."

She stood there a while longer. Then she said, to the river, "Ask me tomorrow."

"Tomorrow?"

"It's Christmas." She still didn't turn around. Her ears were burning. "Everybody's nicer on Christmas."

Behind her she heard him laugh. Low, through his nose. The chuff. Seth's laugh, Jacob's, hers.

"Tomorrow," Harry agreed.

---

At three o'clock he stood at the end of the long table in the Cullens' kitchen with Leah in the doorway behind him and every one of them watching, and said it.

"She stays."

Bella put her face down into Renesmee's hair and didn't lift it for a long time. Edward's hands were on her shoulders. Jacob made a sound like a man who'd been underwater for twenty-four hours coming up. Rosalie, by the refrigerator, closed her eyes.

Then he gave them the conditions, one at a time, exactly the way he'd given them to Leah at the river, without leaving anything out and without softening any of it. Charlie. The blood. Two years. Every three months. The road.

When he got to *not her grandfather if he offers,* Bella lifted her head.

"He'd never offer," she said.

"No," Harry agreed. "But you'd have thought about it, Mrs. Cullen. Anybody would. I'm making sure it's not yours to decide."

Bella stared at him. Then, slowly, she nodded.

"Questions," said Harry.

Nobody had any.

"Right," he said. "Merry Christmas. I'll see you in March."

He turned to go. In the doorway, Leah stepped aside to let him by, and as he passed she reached out without looking and took the coffee cup out of his hand — cold, half full, he'd been carrying it around for five hours — and drank what was left of it.

He let her. He didn't even look round.

In the kitchen, nine people and a child watched Leah Clearwater walk out of the house behind him, close enough that their shoulders touched, finishing his coffee.

"I still don't like it," said Jasper.

"You'll get used to it," said Alice, who couldn't see a single thing about any of it, and for the first time in her very long life didn't seem to mind.

Out on the porch, the rain had started again. Leah stood under the eaves while he put up his umbrella. Down the drive, the cedars dripped. Somewhere out toward the 101, she knew, six fifty-yard stretches of road had something new on them overnight that nobody would ever see, and every wolf in two packs knew exactly where they were.

"Tomorrow," she said.

"Tomorrow," Harry said, and held the umbrella out over her, and she stepped under it.
