# Chapter Three: Site Visit

## I.

It was twenty-two miles back to Turnagain, and she felt every one of them.

The cottonwood flat gave way to gravel bars, and the gravel bars gave way to the long brown stretch where the river slowed and spread and the willows crowded down to the water. The sun came up behind her somewhere around the fourth mile and lit the frost on every stone, so the whole riverbed went white and glittering and her shadow ran out ahead of her across it, long and thin and gray. She'd been awake twenty-six hours. She'd run something like forty miles, raced a wolf the size of a pickup truck up the side of a mountain, killed a caribou, and lain on a rock until midnight telling a stranger things she'd never told anybody. Around mile ten her legs quit complaining and went quiet, the kind of quiet that meant they'd make up for it later.

The pouch bumped against her ankle with every stride.

It didn't rattle. She kept waiting for it to. She caught herself listening for it, the way you listen for a noise in your car you're sure you heard once, and it never came. It just rode there against the cord with her clothes, a small soft weight, about as heavy as a pocketful of change.

*If you want me, say my name into it.*

She made herself think about the river. About the cold. About how late she was going to be.

---

She was very late.

The sun was well up over the mountains by the time she came down through the spruce above the lake road and phased behind the deadfall where she'd left in the morning, and the town below her was awake. Pickups at the gas station. Smoke going straight up from every chimney into air so still she could hear somebody splitting wood on the far side of the bay. The yellow floatplane was gone from its dock, and a thin skin of ice along the shore had already started to go gray and rotten in the sun.

She dressed fast, shivering out of habit more than cold. Jeans. Flannel. The hoodie with the cuffs gone stiff from river water. Her hair was a lost cause, so she twisted it into a rope and stuffed it under the blue toque with the white pompom and didn't let herself think about what was in it.

The pouch was last. She sat on the deadfall and worked the knot loose from the cord with her fingers, and the drawstring came free easily, as if it had been waiting for her to ask.

The mirror inside was smaller than she'd expected. About the size of a playing card, in a plain frame of dark, old silver gone black in the corners, the glass a little cloudy at the edges the way antique mirrors got. It was heavier than it looked. The back was smooth, with no maker's mark and no engraving, nothing.

She tipped it up and looked at herself.

A woman in a stupid hat looked back at her, with twigs in her eyebrows and a smear of something on her jaw that she really hoped was mud. Dark circles under her eyes. A face that looked older than twenty, and thinner than it had three weeks ago, and somehow less tired than it had looked in any mirror in La Push for a year and a half.

Nothing else. No green eyes in the glass. No English voice. Just her.

She held it there a second longer than she needed to. Then she put it back in the pouch and pulled the drawstring tight and shoved the whole thing down into the front pocket of her jeans, where it sat against her hip like a stone.

She told herself she was keeping it there because the lock on room six was a joke. Wes could open it with a credit card. Wes probably had.

She ran down the hill.

---

She came in the back door of the Galena at seven forty-two by the clock over the pass-through, and Dot didn't look up from the grill.

"Your pans are in the sink," Dot said. "They've been in the sink since six."

"I know."

"Clock's on the wall, Washington."

"I know. I'm sorry."

Dot cracked two eggs one-handed onto the flat-top, then two more, and only then looked over. Her eyes went from the toque to the hoodie to the flannel underneath it, which Leah realized, too late, she'd buttoned one hole off all the way down, so that one side of the collar stood up higher than the other like a dog with one ear cocked. They went down to her boots, which were wet to the laces, and back up to her face.

"Hm," said Dot.

"I went for a run."

"I didn't ask."

"I'm just saying."

"You eat?"

Leah thought about caribou. "Yeah."

"Hm," Dot said again, and when Leah came back from the sink ten minutes later with her sleeves rolled up and her arms already red to the elbow, there was a plate on the steel shelf by the dish rack with three fried eggs and a stack of toast on it, which nobody had ordered and Dot didn't mention.

Leah ate it standing up, between racks.

---

Brynn came in at eight with her cheeks pink from the cold and her bun falling down, took one look at Leah through the pass-through, and came straight through the swinging door into the kitchen with her mouth open.

"Oh my *God.*"

"Don't."

"You didn't go home last night."

"I went for a run."

"Your shirt's buttoned wrong."

Leah looked down. She'd meant to fix that. "I was in a hurry."

"You were *in a hurry.*" Brynn's eyes had gone huge. "Wes says your light never came on. Wes says he sat up watching hockey and your light never came on all night, and then this morning you come running down the lake road with your hair in a hat and your shirt on wrong — "

"Why does Wes know when my light comes on?"

"Leah, there are three hundred people in this town and one of them is Wes. Everybody knows everything." Brynn leaned against the steel counter beside the sink and lowered her voice to a whisper that carried to the grill. "Was it the English guy?"

"*No.*"

"Ray says he tried to buy the Prospector."

"Ray needs a hobby."

"Ray says he paid seven hundred dollars for a bottle of Scotch and some pie and then took you down to the *dock.*"

"Brynn."

"I'm just *asking* — "

"Halvorsen," Dot said, without turning around from the grill. "Table four wants coffee. Table four has wanted coffee since you walked in."

Brynn went. At the swinging door she looked back over her shoulder and mouthed, *Later,* with her eyebrows halfway up her forehead.

Leah put her hands in the water and scrubbed a pan she'd already scrubbed.

---

The chef came in at a quarter past ten.

She smelled him from the dock, which meant she smelled him before the bell over the door went: diesel and lake water and the inside of a boat, butter and garlic and expensive olive oil, a fabric softener that came in a big jug, and over all of it, so strong it made her mouth water through the dishwater and the bleach, chocolate. A lot of chocolate. More chocolate than she had ever smelled in one place in her life.

The bell jingled. Through the pass-through she watched a skinny man somewhere in his thirties come in sideways, backing through the door, because he had his arms wrapped around a wooden crate the size of a microwave oven. He wore a black puffy parka unzipped over a white double-breasted chef's jacket, and a Canucks toque, and the expression of a man who hadn't slept in three days and had come to terms with it. He set the crate down on the counter so carefully it didn't make a sound, then stood there with both hands flat on the lid, breathing.

The old men at the corner table had stopped talking about hockey.

"Hi," Brynn said. "Um. Can I get you a menu?"

"Which one of you is Leah?"

Brynn turned around and looked at the pass-through with her whole face lit up. Leah put down the pan.

She came out drying her hands on her apron. Up close, the chef had a narrow, clever face with a lot of stubble on it, and dark eyes, and a burn scar across the back of one hand, the shiny old kind every line cook had somewhere. He looked at her: the toque, the wrong buttons, the steam coming off her forearms. Something in his face eased.

"He said you'd be the one at the sink," the chef said, "who looks like she wants to kill somebody."

"That's me."

"Thank Christ." He held out his hand over the crate. "Theo Abad. I work for — I'm the — " He stopped, and seemed to give up on finishing the sentence. "Lodge. Across the bay. I cook."

She shook his hand. "You're the one who orders eight breakfasts."

"I'm a classically trained chef," Theo said, with great dignity. "I did two years in Lyon. I have cooked for a premier." He paused. "He likes Dot's eggs better than mine. He says mine are very good but hers have *character.*" He patted the crate. "This is yours. Please take it. I need it out of my kitchen."

"What is it?"

"Eleven kilos of Belgian chocolate."

Brynn made a noise.

"He brought it back yesterday morning," Theo said. "Came in the back door at six a.m. smelling like a swamp, with this under one arm, and said, 'Theo, guard this with your life, it's for Leah,' and then went upstairs and had a shower and came straight back down and tried to eat it." He looked at Leah with the haunted eyes of a man who has seen things. "Twice. I had to stand over it with a cleaver. Have you ever told your employer you were going to cut his hand off?"

"What'd he say?"

"He laughed. He said, 'Fair enough.' He said it was a reasonable boundary." Theo shook his head slowly. "Then I went out to the walk-in for ten minutes, and when I came back he was standing in the pantry with the lid off and his whole arm in the box." He lowered his voice. "And he wouldn't eat breakfast. I made him eggs, I made him bacon, I made a whole loaf of bread. He said no thank you, he was saving himself for dinner. Who *saves* himself? He eats like a — " Theo made a vague, despairing shape with both hands. "Where does it *go?*"

Leah thought about the caribou. "No idea."

"Anyway. He came through again at five this morning for about four minutes, ate the risotto I made last night that nobody showed up for, cold, out of the pot, standing up, and left. He's gone till the weekend. Some job. He said if you asked, I should tell you Kazakhstan, and that it's very flat." Theo stepped back from the crate, both hands up, the way you'd step back from a bomb. "And he said to tell you there's a card."

There was. It was tucked under the twine on the lid, a square of thick cream paper folded once, and when Leah opened it the ink was black and blotchy and slanted, like it had been written in a hurry with something that leaked. Somebody had held the pen in their fist.

*Clearwater —*

*As promised. The hazelnut ones are the ones. Do not give Dot more than half.*

*— H.*

*P.S. The family's getting their shop back. Hargreaves is drafting something. He used the word "unprecedented" four times on the phone and I think he's having a nice time.*

She read it twice. Then she folded it and put it in her back pocket, and found Brynn staring at her from six inches away.

"Who's *H?*"

"Nobody."

"*Nobody* bought you eleven kilos of chocolate?"

"I didn't ask for it."

"That's not the *point,* Leah — "

The twine came off. Leah lifted the lid, and the smell came up out of the crate and filled the whole café like a wave coming in over a seawall, and the old men at the corner table actually leaned over in their chairs.

Inside, packed in wood shavings and stiff waxed paper, were boxes. Dozens of them. Flat gold boxes and tall brown ones tied with ribbon, little cellophane bags of something dusted in cocoa, a tin with a picture of a cathedral on it, and in every one of them, rows of chocolates, dark and milk and white, swirled and ridged and dusted and shaped like seashells, each one sitting in its own pleated paper cup like an egg in a carton. Gold lettering on every lid: *Chocolaterie Van Acker — Bruxelles — depuis 1952.*

Brynn put a hand over her mouth.

"Hazelnut," Leah said. She found them in a gold box near the top, a row of fat little domes with a single toasted hazelnut pressed into each one. She held the box out to Brynn first, which surprised both of them.

Brynn took one and put it in her mouth and closed her eyes, and her face did something Leah had only ever seen on people in church.

"Oh my God," Brynn said, through chocolate. "Oh my *God.* Leah, marry him."

"I'm not marrying anybody."

"Then I'll marry him. Is he single? Does he like nursing students? Tell him I'm very hardworking — "

Leah ate one herself, to shut her up.

It broke under her teeth with a snap, and then went soft, and then there was the hazelnut, and some kind of praline under it that tasted like toast and caramel and something burnt in the best way, and she stood there in the middle of the Galena Café with her apron on and the chocolate melting on her tongue and had to put one hand on the counter.

It was the best thing she had ever tasted. It was better than the Scotch.

She'd have died before telling him.

"Washington."

Dot was standing in the swinging door with her spatula in one hand and her glasses down her nose, looking at the crate. Then at Theo. Then at Leah.

Leah held out the gold box.

Dot came over, took a hazelnut one, and ate it the way she did everything, without ceremony, in one bite. She chewed. Her face did nothing at all.

"Hm," she said.

Then she reached past Leah into the crate, took out a whole flat gold box, tucked it under her arm, and went back into the kitchen. The door swung shut behind her.

Theo watched her go with a look of naked professional respect. "He said she'd take half."

"She took one box."

"It's early," said Theo. He turned toward the door. Then he turned back. "Is it — sorry. Can I ask you something?"

Leah waited.

"Is he always like this?" Theo said. "He talks about you like you're weather. Like, 'I wonder what Leah's doing,' the way somebody else would say, 'I wonder if it's going to rain.' And then he goes off and does whatever he does and comes back at six in the morning with a box of chocolates and his boots full of swamp." He rubbed the back of his neck. "My wife says I should be scared of him. She's never met him. I just think he's very — " He hunted for the word. "Hungry."

"Yeah," said Leah. "That's about it."

Theo nodded, as if she'd confirmed something about the weather, and went out. The bell jingled. Through the steamed window she watched him walk down to the dock where an aluminum boat with an outboard sat bobbing against the tires, and climb in, and pull the cord three times before it caught, and go off across the bay in a long white wake toward the green roof on the far shore.

She stood there for a while after he'd gone.

Then she shut the lid on eleven kilos of chocolate, carried the whole crate back to the kitchen, and put it on the shelf over the dish rack where she could see it.

Dot didn't say a word about it all day. But around two o'clock, when Leah went to get a hazelnut one, she found that the second gold box was gone too.


---

## II.

She called her mother on Saturday, the way she'd promised.

It was a short call. Seth was out with Jacob. Mom was on her way to Charlie Swan's with a casserole and had her keys in her hand; Leah could hear them jingling down the line. They talked for nine minutes about nothing — the weather in La Push, which was rain, and the weather in Turnagain, which was ice on the puddles every morning now, and how the café was, and whether Leah was eating. Leah said the café was fine. She said she was eating. She did not say anything about eleven kilos of chocolate sitting on a shelf over a dish rack.

"You sound tired," Sue said.

"I work at six."

"Mm-hm."

"Mom."

"I didn't say anything."

"You said *mm-hm.*"

"I'm allowed," her mother said, and Leah could hear her smiling again, and hung up before she had to hear whatever came next.

---

She smelled him at twenty to six on Sunday morning.

She was already awake. She'd been awake since five, lying on the orange bedspread in the dark with the window cracked open and the cold coming in over the sill, listening to the heater not work. The café was closed. Nobody needed her anywhere. She'd been trying to decide whether to run or sleep, and losing the argument both ways, when the wind came across the parking lot and through the gap at the bottom of the window and brought it in with it.

Earth after rain. Pine. Cold wet stone.

Lake water and outboard exhaust and the plastic smell of new things still in their packaging.

She lay very still on the bedspread and listened to her own heart do something stupid.

Footsteps crossed the gravel. Real ones, heavy and unhurried, boots crunching on the frost, loud enough that anybody in any of Wes's rooms would hear them. She thought about the plateau, and the cottonwood, and a thing the size of a truck coming down off a branch onto a foot of dead leaves without making a sound. He could walk up to her door so quietly she'd never know he was there until he was breathing on her ear. He was making noise so she'd hear him coming.

The footsteps stopped outside room six.

He knocked. Three times, not loud.

Leah got up. She didn't fix her hair. She opened the door.

He was standing on the strip of concrete under the buzzing light in the most normal clothes she'd seen him wear yet: a navy wool sweater with a hole in one elbow, old canvas trousers gone soft at the knees, and a pair of green rubber boots that came halfway up his shins. No coat. The cold didn't seem to have occurred to him. His hair looked like he'd slept on it wet. Under one arm were two fishing rods, still in their plastic sleeves with the price tags on them, and in his other hand was a red metal tackle box with a sticker on the lid that said *TURNAGAIN MERCANTILE $34.99,* and a steel thermos was tucked into the crook of his elbow.

"Morning," said Harry Potter. "Third date?"

She looked at him. "I said maybe."

"I know. I've come to find out which way maybe went."

She looked at the rods. "Those still have the tags on."

"Bought them yesterday. In Vancouver. Then I got back in the afternoon and went into the Mercantile, and there was a bloke called Earl, and I said I'd like everything I'd need to catch a fish in that lake, and he looked at me for a very long time and sold me this." He lifted the tackle box. "He said I'd want the red-and-white ones."

"You don't fish."

"I've never been fishing in my life," he agreed. "I thought you might know how."

Leah stood in the doorway in her socks.

She'd told him about the boat. Blue hull, rust streak down the bow. She'd told him on the rock, in the dark, about a man who taught her to gut a fish when she was six. He'd heard every word of it. And now here he was on Wes's concrete at a quarter to six in the morning with a pair of rods he didn't know how to use.

"Is this because of my dad?" she said. It came out harder than she meant.

He didn't look away. "Partly."

"*Partly.*"

"You talked about him on that rock like he was the best thing that ever happened to you," Harry said. "And I've got a lake and a boat and no idea what to do with either of them. Seemed like there might be a day in that." He shifted the tackle box to his other hand. "If it's the wrong day, say. We'll do something else. Or nothing. I'll go and eat Dot's door off its hinges and wait till Monday."

She opened her mouth to tell him it was the wrong day.

She thought about the lake in the early light. About her hands on a tiller. About the smell of an outboard motor at dawn, two-stroke oil and gasoline, which she hadn't smelled since the spring before her father died, the last time she'd gone out with him, when she'd been seventeen and bored and sat in the bow with her headphones on and hadn't known it was the last time.

"Give me five minutes," she said.

"Take ten."

"I said five," Leah said, and shut the door in his face.

---

The boat was a fourteen-foot aluminum skiff with three bench seats, a dented hull, and a twenty-five-horse Evinrude on the back that looked older than she was. He'd tied it to the motel's end of the town dock, badly. Leah looked at the knot for a while.

"What?" he said.

"Nothing."

"It's a perfectly good knot."

"It's a granny knot. It's a granny knot on top of another granny knot. My grandma would be embarrassed by that knot."

"It held," he said, with dignity, and stepped down into the boat, and the boat took his weight like he was a sack of feathers. Not a wobble. Not even a dip at the gunwale. It sat there on the flat gray water as if nobody had gotten in at all.

Leah looked at that for a while too.

"Bit of a trick," he said, catching her look. "Same as the branch."

"Do you ever just — weigh things? Like a normal person?"

"Only when I'm trying to make a point." He sat down on the middle bench. The boat still didn't move. "In you get."

She got in. The boat rocked under her like a boat was supposed to, and she felt better about everything.

He drove first. That lasted about forty seconds. He pulled the cord and the Evinrude coughed and caught, and he twisted the throttle the wrong way and then the right way all at once, and the bow came up out of the water and swung hard to the left toward the pilings of the dock, and Leah grabbed the gunwale with one hand and the tiller with the other and yanked them back out into open water with about a foot to spare.

They sat there idling, both looking at the dock.

"Move," said Leah.

"I had that."

"*Move.*"

He moved. She took the tiller. The handle was cold and rough under her palm, the rubber grip cracked, and the engine thrummed up through the hull and into the soles of her boots and the bones of her legs, and she opened it up and pointed them out into the middle of the lake, and the wind came off the water into her face so cold it made her eyes run.

She didn't wipe them. She let them run.

He sat in the bow facing her, with the tackle box between his feet and the rods across his knees and the wind making his hair worse, and he didn't say anything at all for a long time. She was grateful for that. She didn't look at him. She looked at the water, and the mountains, and the long gray reach of the lake opening out ahead of them with mist lying on it in patches like smoke on a battlefield, the sun just starting to come gold over the eastern ridge and burn holes in it.

---

There was a point of land about four miles up the east shore where the bottom came up out of deep water onto a long shelf of broken rock. She could see it from a long way off: the color of the water went from black to green to brown, and the ripples changed shape where the wind came over the shallows. Her dad had taught her that, a long time ago, in a different country, on a different kind of water. Fish came up where the bottom came up. In the fall, they came up shallow.

She cut the engine and let them drift.

The quiet came down over them all at once, the way it does when a motor stops. The water slapping the hull. A loon somewhere off to the north. The tick of the engine cooling.

"Give me those," she said.

He gave her the rods. She tore the plastic off with her teeth, and the price tags, and spat them into the bottom of the boat. Cheap spinning reels, ten-pound line already spooled on. Earl had sold him the right stuff, more or less. In the tackle box was a jumble of hooks and sinkers and swivels and a whole tray of spoons, the long curved shiny lures shaped like the bowl of a spoon with the handle snapped off, most of them red and white striped.

"Earl's not stupid," she said.

"He seemed very sensible. He asked me if I'd ever seen a fish before."

"What'd you say?"

"I said yes, on a plate." He watched her tie a swivel onto the end of his line, fast, the knot her dad had taught her, wrap and wrap and through and back and cinch. "What's that one called?"

"Improved clinch."

"Can you teach me that?"

"No," said Leah, and handed him the rod. "Cast."

"How?"

She showed him. Bail open, line under the finger, rod back over the shoulder, and then forward, smooth, and let go of the line at the top. Her spoon went out over the water in a long flat arc, glinting, and landed with a small *plip* sixty feet off the side of the boat. She closed the bail and started to reel.

He watched her do it. He nodded slowly. He opened his bail. He put the line under his finger. He drew the rod back over his shoulder.

And let go of the line behind his head.

The spoon went straight up into the air, came down behind him, and hooked itself neatly into the back of his own collar.

There was a pause.

"Right," he said.

Leah had to put her rod down. She folded over on the back bench with her face in her hands, and the laugh came out of her the same way it had in the pumpkin field, the same way it had in the Prospector, without asking her first. She laughed until the boat rocked. She laughed until her stomach hurt and her eyes started running again, and when she finally looked up he was sitting there in the bow with the red-and-white spoon dangling from the back of his sweater, looking at her with his head tilted, quiet, watching her like he wanted to remember it.

She stopped.

"Hold still," she said, and leaned over and unhooked him.

His second cast went straight down into the water beside the boat with a sound like a dropped spoon. His third went forty feet in roughly the right direction, which was something, and then the line came off the reel all at once in a great springing mess of loops and snarls, a bird's nest the size of a grapefruit, and the spoon sank to the bottom of the lake and stayed there.

He looked at the tangle for a while. His eyes went narrow and very slightly unfocused.

"Don't," said Leah.

"I wasn't."

"You were about to."

"I was going to *think* about it."

"You do one magic thing on this boat," Leah said, "and I swear to God I'm throwing you in."

"I'd float."

"You'd sink like a rock."

"I'd float out of spite," Harry said, but he stopped looking at the tangle, and held the rod out to her with an expression of total surrender. She took it and sat there in the stern and picked the knots out one by one with her fingernails, the way she'd done a hundred times for Seth when he was little and couldn't cast to save his life either, and he sat across from her and watched her hands.

---

She got the first one an hour later, and it nearly took the rod out of her hands.

She was trolling. They'd given up on casting, for his sake, and she'd started the motor again at its slowest and run them in long lazy lines up and down the edge of the shelf with both rods out the back, the spoons wobbling along twenty feet down, the way you trolled for salmon off La Push. The rod tip on her side dipped once. Twice. Then it went down hard and stayed down, bent nearly double, and line began to come off the reel with a long rising scream.

"Oh," said Harry. "Oh, is that — "

"Shut up."

She cut the motor and stood up in the stern and played it. Rod up, reel down, rod up, reel down. It ran on her three times. Each time she let it go, let the drag hum, and felt the weight of it pulling deep and slow and stubborn under the boat, shaking its head, and each time it slowed she brought it back up, a few feet at a time. Her arms burned. The cold went out of her hands. It was twelve minutes by the watch she didn't have, and it was the best twelve minutes she'd had since she was seventeen.

Then it came up beside the boat, rolling, and she saw it.

Lake trout. Long and heavy and dark green-gray along the back, its sides covered in pale cream spots like snow on a dark window, its belly gone a deep orange. Its fins were edged with white. She got her hand under its belly and lifted it over the gunwale, carefully, both hands, and set it down on the wet floor of the boat between them, where it lay heaving, nearly three feet of it, so heavy her forearms shook.

Harry was staring at it with his mouth open.

"That's a fish," he said.

"That's a fish."

"That's an *enormous* fish."

"Twenty pounds. Maybe more." She was breathing hard. She was grinning. She couldn't help it. She put her hand on its side, on the soft swollen belly, and felt it. Felt how round it was, how tight. And her grin went quieter.

She bent down, got her fingers under its gill plate the way her dad had taught her — never in the gills, never squeeze — and her other hand under its tail, and lifted it, and leaned out over the side of the boat, and put it back in the lake.

She held it there in the water. Moving it, very slowly, forward and back, so the water ran through its gills. It lay in her hands like a log, stunned. Then its tail moved. Then again. Then it came alive all at once, and twisted out of her grip, and went down into the green and the brown and the dark and was gone.

She sat back on the bench with her arms soaking to the elbow.

He hadn't said anything. When she finally looked up, he was watching her the same way he'd watched her laugh.

"Female," Leah said. "Full of eggs. They come up shallow in the fall to spawn. You let the big mamas go." She wiped her hands on her jeans. "That's the rule. My dad's rule. You let the big mamas go so there's fish next year, and you keep the little guys for supper."

"Good rule," said Harry.

"Yeah."

"I'd never have known."

"I know you wouldn't." She looked at the place where the fish had gone. "That's why there's rules."

---

She got a smaller one after that, a male, a buck, maybe five pounds, and kept it, and hit it once on the head with the handle of a screwdriver from the tackle box so it wouldn't suffer. And twenty minutes after that the rod on Harry's side went down, and he grabbed it so fast the boat actually rocked, and stood up in the bow, and fought it like it was a dragon.

It was a lake trout. It was about eleven inches long.

He held it up in both hands, dripping, in the morning sun, and turned to her with an expression on his face she wouldn't have believed if she hadn't seen it. A twenty-six-year-old man who'd turned a hundred-and-thirty-year-old killer into dust with his fist, who owned houses he couldn't count and a chocolate shop by accident, holding an eleven-inch fish with his eyes lit up like a kid on Christmas morning.

"Clearwater," he said. "*Look* at it."

"I see it."

"I caught a *fish.*"

"You caught a baby."

"It's a *fish.*" He turned it over in his hands, studying it. "It's got spots. Look at its little spots."

"Put it back."

"It's my fish."

"It's a baby, Potter. Put it back. Rules."

He looked at the fish for a moment longer. Then he leaned out over the side of the boat, and held it in the water exactly the way she'd held hers, fingers under the gill plate, never squeezing, moving it forward and back, and waited until it twisted out of his hands and went down out of sight.

He sat back down on the bench with his sleeves dripping. "I'm going to remember that fish for the rest of my life," he said, quite seriously.

Leah looked at him, and something in her chest turned over that had nothing to do with the wolf.

---

There was a gravel beach in the lee of the point, a little half-moon of gray stones with spruce coming down to it and a fringe of yellow willows, out of the wind, catching the sun. She ran the skiff up onto it until the bow grated, and stepped out into six inches of water so cold it should have stopped her heart, and pulled the boat up with one hand.

He'd brought lunch. That was to say, Theo had sent lunch, in a wicker hamper with leather straps, and when Harry opened it on the stones she stood there and looked at it for a long moment.

"Is that a tablecloth?"

"I told him a picnic," Harry said. "He heard 'Lyon.'"

There was a tablecloth. White linen, folded in a square. There was a baguette, a wedge of cheese that smelled like feet, a jar of little pickles, a whole roast chicken wrapped in foil, a box of pastries, a bottle of white wine with the cork pulled and stuck back in, two glasses wrapped in napkins, and, at the very bottom, in a paper bag with *DOT* written on it in Theo's handwriting, six slices of saskatoon pie.

"Theo went to Dot's?" Leah said. "Dot's closed on Sundays."

"He went yesterday. He said she looked at him like something on her shoe and then sold him the whole pie for forty dollars." Harry pulled out the chicken. "He's in love with her, I think. Professionally."

They built a fire out of driftwood and willow sticks. She did it; he watched. She could feel him wanting to light it with a look, and he didn't, and she got it going on the second match. Then she took the buck trout down to the edge of the water and knelt on the stones, and he came and crouched beside her and held out a knife without being asked. A plain folding knife with a wooden handle, the kind you could buy at any hardware store.

"Earl?" she said.

"Earl."

She opened it. She turned the fish belly-up in her left hand, slid the point into the vent, and ran the blade up the belly toward the gills in one long, shallow stroke, keeping the edge turned up so it wouldn't cut anything it shouldn't. She cut the throat latch. She hooked two fingers into the gills and pulled, and everything came out together in one piece, the way it was supposed to, and she threw it in the lake for the gulls. Then she ran her thumbnail down the dark line of blood along the spine, inside the cavity, once, hard, from tail to head, and rinsed it in the lake.

Six years old on the front steps. Her dad's big hand over hers on the knife. *Thumb down the spine, Lee-Lee. That's where the bitter is. You get that out and it tastes like heaven.*

She didn't realize she'd stopped moving until Harry said, quietly, "You all right?"

"Fine." She rinsed the fish again. "He used to call me Lee-Lee. When I was little. Nobody else ever did."

He didn't say anything to that. She was getting used to him not saying anything, and to the way it didn't feel like he was waiting for her to stop talking. He held out his hand for the knife. She gave it to him. He wiped it on his trousers and folded it and handed it back to her.

"Keep it," he said.

"It's a nine-dollar knife."

"Then it's a nine-dollar knife you've used," he said, "and I haven't."

She put it in her pocket, with the mirror.

---

They ate on the tablecloth on the stones with the fire going and the trout cooking in foil in the coals with butter and a lemon from the hamper. They ate the chicken. They ate the bread and the stinky cheese and the pastries, and drank Theo's wine out of Theo's glasses, which was ridiculous, and Leah laughed at how ridiculous it was until she noticed she was enjoying it, and then stopped laughing and kept enjoying it anyway. When the fish came out of the coals it fell off the bone, pink and rich and steaming, and he ate half of it and then sat looking at the other half with the expression he'd had in the boat.

"Go on," she said.

"It's your fish."

"You'll stare at it till I give it to you anyway."

He ate it. She ate the pie.

When it was all gone they sat side by side on the tablecloth with their backs against a big bleached log, close but not touching, with the fire going down and the sun on their faces and the lake lying out in front of them flat and glittering all the way to the far shore.

"Third date," Leah said.

He was quiet for a second. "Yes."

"You said you'd tell me."

"I did." He picked up a flat stone from beside the tablecloth and turned it over in his fingers. "Where d'you want me to start?"

"How are you a wolf?"

"Right," he said. "Well. When I was seventeen, I walked into a forest to die."

Leah turned her head.

His eyes stayed on the stone. His voice was level and ordinary, the voice of a man telling somebody where he'd parked.

"The man I told you about. Voldemort. The one who killed my mum and dad. When he tried to kill me, when I was one, the curse went wrong and he left a bit of himself behind in me. A bit of his soul. I didn't know for sixteen years. And then I found out, and the only way to get it out of me was to let him kill me. So I walked into the forest where he was waiting, and I put my wand in my pocket, and I let him." He turned the stone over. "And he did."

The fire popped. A gull went over.

"I went somewhere afterwards," Harry said. "White. Very quiet. Looked a bit like King's Cross station, if somebody'd cleaned it. I had a conversation with somebody who'd been dead a year. And then I had a choice to go on, or go back. And I went back." He shrugged. "Woke up on the ground in the forest with my face in the pine needles, and he thought I was dead, and I let him think it for a bit. Then I killed him the next morning. In front of everyone."

Leah sat very still against the log.

She was thinking about a chief, a long time ago, who could leave his body and walk around as a spirit. Who went out one night and couldn't get back in. Who wandered for a long time with nowhere to go. And who came back, in the end. With a wolf.

"Yeah," Harry said. She hadn't said a word. He must have seen it on her face. "I thought about that too. On the rock. When you told it."

"Keep going."

"Four years after that, I decided to become an Animagus." He glanced at her. "It's a wizard who can turn into an animal. One animal, always the same one, whenever they like. It's hard. Most people never manage it, and most people who try make a terrible mess of themselves. You have to hold a mandrake leaf in your mouth for a month."

"A *month?*"

"A whole month. Full moon to full moon. Eating, sleeping, talking. You can't take it out once, or you start again." He made a face. "I started again three times. I sneezed it out in the shower once and nearly cried."

Leah snorted, and didn't mean to.

"My dad was one," he said. "A stag. And my godfather, he was a dog, a big black dog. I never knew my dad. I knew my godfather for about two years, and then he died too." He turned the stone over again. "I thought if I could do it, I'd be a stag. Like that silver one you saw on the mountain. Like my dad. That was the whole idea. Bit sentimental."

"You weren't a stag."

"I was not a stag." The corner of his mouth went up. "You do it in the end in a thunderstorm. You drink a potion and say some words while the lightning's going, and you change. You're meant to come out exactly the size of the animal. A cat's a cat-sized cat. A dog's a dog-sized dog. There's rules." He tossed the stone in his palm. "I did it in a barn in Devon. And when I changed, I put my head through the hayloft and my back through the roof."

Leah stared at him.

"Ron was there," Harry said. "Ron fell off a hay bale. Hermione screamed. I couldn't get out of the barn without taking the whole side off it, so I took the whole side off it. I had to pay the farmer." He frowned slightly. "I think Hargreaves might have bought the farm. I should check."

"And when you changed back — "

"When I changed back, I kept some things." He touched the side of his nose. "This. I've smelt things nobody else can smell since that night. And the heat. And I can't get cold anymore, even when I want to. And — " He grinned at her, very slightly, and there it was, the long white canine on the left side, sitting in his mouth where no human tooth had any business being. "This, which my dentist has asked me about four times."

"That's not in the rules," Leah said.

"No. You change, and you change back, and you're you again. Nothing comes over. There's nothing like me in any book anyone can find." He tossed the stone into the lake, and it skipped twice and sank. "Hermione has a theory. It's forty pages long and has footnotes. The short version is that when I went to the white place and came back, something came back with me, and it was waiting for me to give it a shape. And when I finally tried to, it picked its own."

"What's Ron's theory?"

"That I'm a freak. It's shorter."

"What do you think?"

He thought about it, which she'd learned meant he was about to tell the truth.

"I think I'm a wolf," he said. "And I don't think about the rest much. It doesn't help." He looked at her sideways. "I don't change for your vampires. Nothing wakes up in me when they're near, or at least it never has. I've never met one. And hearing you in your head has nothing to do with the wolf. That's the mind magic. I could do that before." He paused. "I'm not one of you, Leah. I'd guess I'm a cousin. A long way removed. From a different side of the family."

She thought about that for a long time.

"Show me," she said.

He looked at her.

"The changing." She tipped her chin at the beach. "I've never seen you do it. You were already a wolf in the tree, and you were still one when you left. Show me."

He got up. He walked a few yards down the gravel, away from the fire and the tablecloth, and stood with his back to the lake, facing her, in his navy sweater with the hole in the elbow and his canvas trousers and his green rubber boots. He didn't take anything off. He didn't untie anything.

"Ready?"

"Just do it."

He did it.

She'd phased hundreds of times. She'd watched Seth do it, and Jacob, and Sam, and all of them. It was a violent thing. It always was. A shudder that started in the spine and tore outward, a burst of heat, the body coming apart along every seam at once and slamming back together bigger, and a pop and rip of fabric if you'd been stupid enough not to strip first. It took half a second, and it looked like an explosion, because it was one.

This took less than that. There was a man on the beach, and then there was a wolf the size of a pickup truck, black as a well, standing on the gray stones in the sun with his tail sweeping slowly behind him. The stones under his paws hadn't shifted. Nothing had torn. There wasn't a scrap of navy wool on the beach, or a strip of canvas, or a single green rubber boot.

He tilted his head at her. Then he did it again, back the other way, just as fast.

The man stood there on the stones in his navy sweater with the hole in the elbow and his canvas trousers and his green rubber boots. Not a thread out of place. The hole in the elbow was still there. His hair, if anything, looked slightly better.

Leah sat against the log with her mouth open.

"You get to keep your *pants?*"

"Trousers."

"You get to keep your *clothes?* Every time?"

"Every time. It's an Animagus thing. You take whatever you're wearing with you, and it comes back when you do. Nobody knows why. There's a forty-page — "

"Do you know how many shirts I've lost?" She was on her feet. She didn't remember getting up. "Do you know how many pairs of jeans? I phased in the middle of a Thriftway parking lot in Forks once when I was nineteen, and my mom had just bought me those jeans, they were new, they were fifty dollars, and I had to walk four miles home through the woods naked in the rain, and you just get to — " She waved her hand at him, at the sweater, the trousers, the boots. "*Keep* them?"

"I'm very sorry," he said, and he very obviously wasn't, and she'd have hit him if she could have reached him without walking over there.

So she walked over there. She stood in front of him on the stones with her fists on her hips and her face hot, and looked up at him, because he was taller than her by half a head, and he looked down at her with his eyes creased at the corners and his mouth doing everything it could not to smile.

"Could you do that for me?" she heard herself say. "Whatever it is. The clothes thing."

He stopped trying not to smile. He was quiet a second.

"I don't know," he said. "Maybe. It'd be a charm on your clothes. I've never tried it on anything that changes the way you do." He paused. "I'd want to work it out properly first. On something that isn't your jeans. If you want."

"Maybe," Leah said.

"Maybe," he repeated, and that time he did smile.

---

They put the fire out, and folded Theo's tablecloth, and packed the hamper. The sun had gone around behind the point and the wind had come up off the lake, cold, raising little whitecaps out in the middle where the water was deep.

She stood at the edge of the beach with her hands in her hoodie pocket while he dragged the boat back down the stones into the water, and she thought about the pack.

She'd been thinking about it all day, underneath everything else. Ever since he'd said *I stay on the step outside, by the door,* on the plateau, a week ago. She thought about it every time she checked inside her own head and found it empty. Every time she wondered what it would be like to phase near Seth again, or Jacob. Every time she thought about going home, even for a second, even just to see the beach.

"The thing you do," she said. "The mind thing. Staying on the step."

He straightened up from the boat.

"Can it go the other way? Can somebody learn to keep people out?"

He looked at her for a long moment. She could see him understand, all at once, what she was really asking.

"Yes," he said. "It's called Occlumency. Closing your mind. It's the other side of the same thing." He rested one hand on the bow of the skiff. "Bloke spent the best part of a term trying to teach me when I was fifteen. I was dreadful at it. He hated me and I hated him, and we were both right. I learned it properly later, on my own, when I had to." He paused. "It can be taught. It's mostly practice."

"Could you teach it?"

"I could try." He was watching her. "You'd be good at it. You're halfway there already. You've spent a year and a half keeping things out of the heads of ten blokes who could hear everything you thought. That's more practice than most people get in their whole lives."

She didn't answer that.

"Maybe," she said finally.

"You know," he said, "you say that a lot."

"I'm thinking about it."

"I know you are," Harry said. "That's why I like hearing it."

She drove them back. He sat in the bow with his back to the wind and watched her the whole way, and she let him, and didn't look at him once, and felt it the whole time on the side of her face like sun.

At the town dock she tied the skiff off herself, two half hitches, and stood up on the planks. He stayed in the boat.

"Fourth date?" he said.

Leah looked down at him in the boat, in the last of the light, with his hair blown into a mess and an eleven-inch trout's slime still drying on his sleeves.

"You want a fourth one," she said, "you can wait for me to ask."

He didn't argue. She was starting to count on that.

"With the mirror?"

"If I feel like it."

"Fair," said Harry Potter. He untied her two half hitches with one hand and pushed the boat off from the dock with the other, and she watched him go, chugging out across the bay in the dusk toward the green roof, steering in big wobbly S-curves. He didn't say *see ya later, alligator.* She noticed that he didn't.

She noticed that she'd been waiting for it.


---

## III.

"Monday," Dot said on Friday, scraping the flat-top at close. "Two o'clock. We're shut, but I do a turkey for whoever hasn't got anywhere to be. Strays' Thanksgiving." She didn't look up. "You're a stray."

"I know."

"Bring something if you want. Don't bring anything if you don't. Wear a shirt with the buttons done right."

Leah went back to room six that night and sat on the edge of the orange bedspread with the mirror in her hands for twenty minutes.

The moose watched her from its lake. She told it to mind its own business.

Then she said, "Potter," to the glass, feeling like an idiot.

Nothing happened for about three seconds. Then her own face fogged over, as if somebody had breathed on the glass from the other side, and cleared again on his face. Too close and lit orange from below, by firelight, with something white and blowing behind his head that she realized was snow. His hair was full of it.

"Clearwater," he said. He didn't sound surprised. He sounded like a man who'd been carrying a mirror in his pocket all week.

"Where are you?"

"Tent. Mountain. Kazakhstan again. It's very flat at the bottom and very much not at the top." Something howled behind him, the wind or something else. "Everything all right?"

"Dot does a Thanksgiving on Monday. At the café. For strays." She made herself say the rest of it. "You eat like a stray. You want to come?"

On the other side of the glass, his face did something she'd have paid money to see again, and she'd only just seen it.

"What should I bring?" he said.

"You're asking."

"I said I would."

"Ask Dot," said Leah.

"Can Theo come? He can't get a flight out till Wednesday and he's started talking to the fish in the freezer."

"Ask *Dot,*" Leah said again, and put the mirror face down on the bedspread before he could see what her face was doing, and sat there on the edge of the bed until it stopped.

---

He asked Dot. Leah knew because on Saturday morning there was a sound from the back door of the café like somebody knocking politely, and Dot went to see who it was and stood there in the doorway for a full minute with the cold coming in around her, and then said, "Ice. Two bags. And the cook can do pies if he wants. He's not touching my turkey," and shut the door.

"Who was that?" Brynn said.

"Nobody," said Dot and Leah, at the same time, and then looked at each other.

---

On Monday he brought two bags of ice.

That was all. He came up the street on foot at five to two in a dark wool overcoat with the collar turned up, and the snow that had been threatening all morning was just starting to come down out of a sky the color of an old dishrag, big slow flakes that melted the second they touched the sidewalk. He had a twenty-pound bag of ice from the gas station in each hand, and he walked into the Galena and set them on the counter, and stood back, and looked at Dot.

Dot looked at the ice. Then at him.

"Hm," she said, and Leah, behind the pass-through with a potato masher in her hand, saw something happen at the corner of Dot's mouth that might have been the beginning of a smile, if Dot had been the kind of woman who did that.

Theo came in behind him with four pies stacked in a cardboard box, and went straight through to the kitchen with the look of a man walking into a duel.

They'd pushed every table in the place together down the middle of the room into one long crooked line and covered it with three different tablecloths. The windows were steamed over solid. There was a turkey in the oven that Dot had put in at six that morning, and the whole café smelled like it, and like sage and onion and butter and the cranberries bubbling on the back burner. The radio on the shelf over the coffee machine was playing an old-timey country station out of Whitehorse that came in and out like a tide.

The strays came in out of the snow one by one. Wes from the motel, in a clean shirt with his ponytail combed. Ray, carrying a case of Molson and looking at Harry like he was expecting him to try to buy the café. Constable Trudel, out of uniform, in jeans and a fleece, with a bottle of red wine and a face that said she'd been on shift since six and wasn't about to discuss it. Three of the old men from the corner table — Gus, Ted, and another one everybody called Moose for reasons nobody explained. Brynn, and Brynn's mother Lorraine, who had a casserole and a lot of questions she was clearly saving for later.

And at twenty past two, when everybody had sat down, the bell over the door jingled one more time, and Dale came in.

The table went quiet.

Dale stood just inside the door with snow on the shoulders of his camo jacket and a pumpkin pie in a plastic clamshell from the grocery store in Dease Lake, with the price sticker still on it, held out in front of him in both hands like he was afraid it might go off. He was sober. He looked like he'd shaved for it. He looked down the long crooked table and found Leah and went a dull, blotchy red from his collar to his hairline.

"Dot," he said. "Said I could come."

"I said you could come if you behaved," said Dot.

"I'm behaving." He swallowed. He was looking at Leah. "Hey. I, uh. Couple weeks ago. I was out of line. At the bar. I'm sorry."

Leah looked at him. At the pie. At the sticker on the pie, which said $6.49.

"Okay," she said.

He blinked. He seemed to have been braced for a lot more than *okay.*

"Okay," Dale said back, and sat down at the far end of the table next to Moose, as far from her as he could get without leaving the building, and put the pie in front of him, and didn't let go of it for the first ten minutes.

Across from Leah, Harry hadn't said a word. When she looked at him he was watching Dale with no expression at all. Then he looked at her, and one corner of his mouth moved.

"Good pie," he said quietly. "Probably."

---

Dot brought the turkey out on a platter the size of a car door and set it down in the middle of the table in a cloud of steam, and everybody made the noise people make. Then she picked up the carving knife and the big two-pronged fork and looked down the table, and her eyes landed on Harry.

"You," she said. "Carve."

The table went quiet again. Even Ray stopped with his beer halfway up.

Leah knew exactly what it was. She'd seen her own grandmother do it to Sam, the first Thanksgiving he came to the Clearwaters' house, with the whole family watching. It was a test. You put the knife in the new guy's hand in front of everybody and found out what he was made of.

Harry stood up. He took the knife and the fork from Dot, and looked at the turkey for a second, the way he'd looked at Kell's binding. Then he set the fork into the breast and took off the leg at the joint in one clean pull, and the wing, and turned the bird and laid the knife flat along the breastbone and brought the whole breast away in one piece. He laid it on the board and sliced it across the grain, even slices a quarter-inch thick, fanned out on the platter, and did the other side, and he didn't talk while he did it, and he didn't hurry, and he didn't make a mess.

When he set the knife down, Dot was looking at the platter.

"Who taught you that?" she said.

"Mrs. Weasley. My best mate's mum." He wiped his hands on a napkin. "Seven kids and a husband who couldn't carve to save his life. She put the knife in my hand the first Christmas I spent with them, when I was fourteen. Said I was the only one in the house who held still long enough to learn."

Dot looked at him for a long moment.

"Sit down," she said. "Eat."

He sat down. Brynn, beside Leah, leaned over and whispered, "Did you *see* that," and Leah said, "Shut up," and passed her the potatoes.

---

They ate. It was the loudest meal Leah had sat through in a year and a half. Gus and Ted argued about whether the Canucks had been robbed in the Bertuzzi trade or had robbed Florida blind, because they'd gotten that new goalie, Luongo, out of it, and Ted said Luongo was the best thing to happen to Vancouver since Expo 86, and Gus said Ted had never been to Vancouver. Constable Trudel told a story about a moose that had walked into the RCMP detachment in Atlin through a door somebody left open and stood in the lobby for two hours. Wes laughed for the first time in Leah's experience, a high, wheezing giggle that made Lorraine Halvorsen look at him with deep concern. Ray drank four beers and started telling everybody that the English guy had paid seven hundred dollars for a bottle of Scotch, and Harry said, "It was a very good bottle," and Ray said, "It was *nine years old,*" and the whole table laughed.

In the kitchen, Theo and Dot were fighting about gravy in low, venomous voices. Leah could hear every word. Theo said something about a roux. Dot said something about Theo's mother.

Harry ate three plates. Leah ate four.

Gus noticed. "Where do you two *put* it?"

"Hollow legs," said Harry, and Leah, at the same time, "Running," and Gus looked at them both with deep suspicion and went back to his turkey.

It was over the pie that Brynn turned to Harry.

She'd been looking at him off and on all through dinner. Leah had noticed. She'd watched Brynn look at Kell, with that dreamy, faraway look Leah had hated, and she was braced for it. What she got was a small frown between Brynn's eyebrows, like she was trying to remember where she'd left her keys.

"Have we met?" Brynn said.

Across the table, Harry's face went completely still.

"I don't think so," he said.

"It's so weird. You totally remind me of something. Like I've seen you before somewhere." Brynn laughed and shook her head. "It's dumb. You remind me of a *deer.* Isn't that weird? I had this dream, a couple weeks ago, when I was sick — "

Under the table, Leah put her heel down on top of Harry's foot as hard as she could.

He didn't flinch. He didn't make a sound. His eyes moved to hers across the table, very briefly.

"I get that a lot," Harry said to Brynn. "It's the nose."

Brynn laughed. Everybody laughed. Lorraine said something about how Brynn had dreamed about a bear once when she had chicken pox, in second grade, a pink one, and Brynn said "*Mom,*" and it was over.

Leah took her foot off his.

---

Afterward, before anybody could leave, Dot stood up at the head of the table with her glass of rye and said, "Right. You know the rule."

"Oh God," said Ray.

"Everybody says one thing. One. Then you can go home." She looked at Harry and Theo and Leah, who were the only three at the table who didn't know the rule. "What you're thankful for. Out loud. One thing. I don't care what. Ray, you start, since you're complaining."

"My liquor license," Ray said immediately. "Which I've still got, no thanks to some people."

Gus said Luongo. Ted said Gus was an idiot and he was thankful he didn't have to live with him. Moose said his hip, which had been replaced in Prince George in March. Wes, after some thought, said the new ice machine, which was coming on Thursday. Lorraine said Brynn. Brynn went pink and said January, and everybody knew what she meant. Constable Trudel said a quiet week, and then looked down the table at nobody in particular and added, "Mostly."

Dale said, "Not getting arrested," and looked at his pie.

Theo, standing in the kitchen doorway with a dish towel over his shoulder, said, "Wednesday's flight," and then, when Dot looked at him, "and the gravy, which was very good," and Dot said, "Hm," and that was the closest thing to a peace treaty the Galena had seen in years.

Then it was Leah.

She hadn't thought about it. She should have. Everybody was looking at her, and she sat there with her fork in her hand and her face going hot, and thought about the pumpkin field. About lying on her back in the dirt with nobody else in her head. About the loons on the lake, and the dish racks, and the orange bedspread, and the green sky.

"Quiet," she said.

Dot nodded, as if it were a perfectly ordinary answer. Then she looked at Harry.

He was sitting with his elbows on the table and his chin on his folded hands. He looked at Leah, across the wreckage of the pies, for long enough that Brynn started to grin.

"Getting asked," he said.

Leah looked down at her plate.

---

It was dark by five. The snow had gotten serious. When they came out of the Galena it was falling straight down out of the sky in the light from the windows, thick and soft and silent, and it was sticking now, an inch of it on the sidewalk and the trucks and the railings, and Main Street had gone quiet in the way only snow could make a street go quiet.

Nobody said anything about where they were walking. They walked down the side of the café to the dock.

The planks were white. Their boots left two lines of prints out to the end, side by side. The lake was black and enormous and the snow went down into it without a sound, and the far shore was gone, and the green roof was gone, and the whole world was the end of a dock and the snow and the dark.

She stood at the end with her hands in her hoodie pocket. He stood beside her.

"Solveig," he said, "sent me a letter."

"A letter."

"We have letters that shout. You open them and they shout at you in the voice of the person who wrote them, and then they set themselves on fire." He was looking out at the snow. "It shouted for nine minutes. I timed it. It used the word 'careless' eleven times and 'Thursday' twice, and it said you'd told her about the deer." He glanced down at her. "So. Thank you for that."

"You're welcome."

"I meant it."

"I know." She kept her eyes on the lake. "Is she going to be okay? Brynn?"

"She's going to be fine. It'll fade. She'll forget she ever dreamed it." He was quiet a moment. "I'll be more careful."

"You said that to Solveig?"

"I said it to Solveig. I'm saying it to you."

The snow came down. It caught in his hair and on the shoulders of his coat and didn't melt for a second or two, and then did. It was melting on her too. They both ran too hot for it to stay.

She turned and looked at him.

He was already looking at her. He'd been looking at her, she realized, the whole time, out of the side of his eye, the way he had in the boat. Snow in his hair. His collar turned up. The light from the café windows coming down the side of the building behind him, so his face was half in shadow, and his eyes, in the half that wasn't, were very green.

She thought about Sam behind the community center, the night of the fireworks, when she was sixteen. About how she'd waited for him to do it, the whole night, and he'd finally done it, and she'd felt like she'd been waiting her whole life.

She was done waiting for people to do things.

She took her hands out of her pocket, grabbed the front of his coat in both fists, pulled him down, and kissed him.

For half a second he didn't move at all. She felt him go still the way he'd gone still at the table when Brynn said *deer*. Then his hand came up and settled along her jaw, big and hot, his thumb just under her ear, and he kissed her back.

He tasted like pie. Saskatoon, and coffee, and something under it she didn't have a name for, sharp, like the air before lightning. He was hot, hot as she was, hotter, and she could feel it coming off him through the coat, through her hoodie, everywhere they touched. His mouth was careful for about two seconds and then it wasn't, and she felt the long canine catch her lower lip, just barely, and the sound that came out of her she was going to deny for the rest of her life, and the sound that came out of him was a low rumble in his chest that she felt more than heard, the same sound he'd made on the plateau when he laughed, gone low and rough.

Her wolf had quit rolling on its back. It was on its feet.

She didn't know how long it went on. The snow kept falling. When she finally let go of his coat she was breathing hard, and so was he, and his forehead was against hers, and there was snow melting off both of them in a cloud of steam like the two of them were standing over a hot spring.

"Well," Harry said, after a while. His voice had gone rough. "That's one way to ask."

"Shut up."

"Shutting up."

She didn't let go of him. She'd let go of his coat, but her hands had found their way to his sides somehow, under the open front of the overcoat, against the wool of whatever was under it, and she could feel him breathing. She stood there with her forehead against his and thought, clearly, the way she'd thought it on the plateau: *Nothing's moving.* The dock was a dock. The lake was a lake. Her mother was still her mother, and Seth was still Seth, and she still wanted to hit him, a little, on principle.

The ground was exactly where it had always been. She was the one who'd moved. On her own feet.

"The door," she said into the space between them.

He lifted his head an inch.

"The thing you said. Occlumency." She made herself step back far enough to look at him. "Teach me."

He looked at her for a long time with the snow coming down between them.

"Tomorrow," he said. "If you want. Up on the plateau, where it's quiet."

"Tomorrow I work."

"After."

"After," Leah agreed.

He stepped back. Snow had piled up on the planks around his boots in a neat circle that hadn't touched him. He looked down at her with his hair full of it, and the corner of his mouth went up, and she saw the canine.

"See ya later, alligator," Harry said.

She'd been waiting for it for a week.

"In a while, crocodile," said Leah.

He didn't vanish this time. There was no crack and no ripple across the lake. He walked back up the dock through the snow like anybody would, leaving prints, and turned up the side of the café, and she listened to his footsteps go off up Main Street until the snow swallowed them.

She stood at the end of the dock until she couldn't feel her face. Which, for her, took a long time.


---

## IV.

"Knock knock," said Harry.

It was the second Thursday of lessons, and they were lying on the slab of gray rock at the head of the caribou basin, both of them in their fur, with the sun going down orange behind the western peaks and the snow lying in long stripes across the tundra below. There'd been six lessons. The first two had been a disaster. She'd tried to do what he described — *put it in a room, give the room a door, put something boring in front of the door* — and every time she got the room built, he'd say something, and she'd think something back at him in words, and the whole thing would fall down like a house of cards.

On the third night she'd figured out what to put in the room. Everything. Turnagain. The café. The mine. Kell's empty coat. The tree, the boat, the dock, the snow. Him.

On the fourth night she'd figured out what to put in front of the door.

*Who's there,* she thought.

"Nobody. That's the problem." His ears tipped toward her. "I'm sat on the step and there's a door, and the door's shut, and in front of the door there's a moose."

*Mm-hm.*

"It's a very badly painted moose."

*It's guarding the door.*

"Its front legs bend the wrong way at the knee."

*It's a good moose. Leave it alone.*

"I'm pushing," he said, and she felt him do it. A pressure, faint, at the front of her head, like a hand flat against a door. He'd shown her what it felt like the first night, with her permission, and she'd hated it so much she'd snarled at him for ten minutes. She didn't snarl now. She thought about the moose. She thought about its stupid legs, and its stupid lake, and the stupid sunset behind it, in words, at length, the way you'd recite something boring to yourself in a dentist's chair, and behind the moose the door stayed shut.

The pressure went away.

"Nothing," Harry said. "Moose."

She lifted her head off her paws.

"I mean it. I can hear you narrating the moose. That's all. Everything behind it's gone." He sounded pleased in a way he didn't usually let himself sound. "It took me two years to do that properly, Leah. You've done it in nine days."

*I've had practice.*

"You have." The tip of his tail moved. "Doesn't mean you didn't do it."

She put her chin back down on her paws and looked out at the basin, and let herself be pleased, a little, where he could see it.

---

She called home the Saturday before Halloween. Seth answered on the second ring.

"*Leah?*"

"Hey, kid."

"Oh my *God.* Oh my God, hang on — no, Mom's not here, she's at Charlie's, she's always at Charlie's now — Leah, I think Mom has a *boyfriend* — "

"I know."

"You *know?*"

"I've known for a month. She says *mm-hm* at me on the phone."

Seth made a noise that was half outrage and half laughing. Then he didn't say anything for a second, and she could hear him breathing on the other end, the old beige phone, the kitchen, the rain on the window over the sink. She could almost smell it.

"Quil and Embry came over," Seth said. "To Jake. Like, two weeks after you left. It's five of us now. Well. Four. Five with you." His voice wobbled on the last part and he covered it up fast. "And Nessie's huge, Lee. Renesmee. She's six weeks old and she looks like she's one, and she *talks.* Full sentences. And she does this thing where she touches your face and shows you stuff — "

"She what?"

"Pictures. In your head. Like, she just *shows* you. It's actually really cool." A pause. "You'd hate it."

"Probably."

"Rosalie hates me. Emmett owes me forty-one dollars. Jake sleeps on their couch. Bella can arm-wrestle Emmett and win." He was talking faster and faster, the way he did when he was nervous. "And Sam's okay. Everybody's okay. It's just, like, normal. Weird normal."

"Seth."

"It's just — " He stopped. When he started again his voice was smaller. "It's weird without you. Nobody yells at me. And when I phase, I keep — I keep checking. For you. Like, I'll be on patrol and I'll go to say something to you and you're not there. Jake says it's just distance. I know it's just distance." He swallowed. "I just keep checking."

Leah stood with her forehead against the steel box at the gas station and closed her eyes.

She thought about the pumpkin field. About lying on her back in the dirt with the silence ringing in her head, laughing, while Seth stood in a kitchen eighteen hundred miles south and thought she was dead.

She thought about the moose.

"Seth," she said. "Give me a week."

---

She told Dot on Friday, at close.

Dot listened with her arms folded and the unlit cigarette between her fingers. Then she looked at the calendar on the wall by the walk-in, the one from the feed store in Dease Lake with a picture of a horse on it.

"Told you," Dot said. "Gone by Halloween."

"Halloween was Tuesday."

Dot looked at the calendar again for a long moment.

"Hm," she said, and went into the walk-in, and came out with a whole saskatoon pie in a cardboard box tied with string, and put it on the counter in front of Leah, and went back to scraping the grill. That was all. When Leah said thank you, Dot said, "Bring the box back," without turning around, and Leah understood that this was the nicest thing Dot was ever going to say to her.

Brynn cried, and hugged her, and made her promise to send a postcard of the ocean. "A *real* one. Not a cold one."

"They're all cold."

"Leah."

"Fine," Leah said, and hugged her back.

---

He was waiting at the end of the dock at dawn on Saturday.

The lake had started to freeze. Not in the middle — the middle was still black and open — but along the shore a sheet of new ice had come out twenty yards from the beach overnight, clear as window glass, and the dock pilings stood in it like legs in a frozen pond. The yellow floatplane was gone for the winter. The green roof across the bay had no smoke coming out of its chimney. Theo had flown out on the Wednesday after Thanksgiving with a box of Dot's pie and a look on his face like a man leaving a war.

He was in the long gray coat with the silver clasps. Work clothes. She knew what that meant.

"Where?" she said.

"Mongolia. Then I don't know. They don't tell me till I get there." He looked at the grocery bag tied to the cord around her wrist, with the pie box sticking out of the top. "You're going home."

"Yeah."

"Where's home?"

She looked at him.

"Home," she said.

He looked back at her for a second. Then he nodded. "Fair."

"I want to be there by myself for a while," Leah said. "On my own. I want to see if it holds. If I can walk in the door and not — " She didn't finish. She didn't need to. "Don't come find me."

"How long's a while?"

"I'll tell you."

"With the mirror."

"With the mirror." She reached into her pocket and touched it, through the denim, and the nine-dollar knife beside it. "And the clothes thing. You said you'd work it out."

"I'm working on it. Two pairs of Earl's best jeans have died for science." He made a face. "One of them caught fire. I've not told Earl."

She laughed. It came out shaky. She stepped in and put her arms around him inside the gray coat, and he put his around her, and they stood like that at the end of the dock with the new ice ticking and settling around the pilings, and she breathed him in. Earth after rain. Pine. Cold stone. Struck match. She wanted to keep it somewhere she could find it.

When she kissed him it was slower than on the dock in the snow. He let her take her time.

"See ya later, alligator," he said into her hair.

"In a while, crocodile," said Leah, and let go, and walked up the dock without looking back, because if she looked back she'd stay.

Behind her, a while later, after she'd gone around the side of the café, there was a crack like a rifle shot, and the new ice along the whole shore of the bay rang like a bell.


---

## V.

She ate Dot's pie on the first night, on a rock above a lake on the plateau, with her paws holding the box steady. She flattened the box afterward with her nose and tied it in with her clothes, because Dot had said to bring it back, and somewhere around the Fraser she realized what that meant and had to stop running for a minute.

It took her five days going south. It was downhill, more or less, and she knew the way. The Fraser canyon, with the sockeye gone and the fish ladders empty and the river running low and green. The pine country, gold and bare. The border, where the gray jay was gone from the top of the obelisk and there was snow on the cut instead, and she stepped over the line back into the United States the same way she'd left it, one back paw and then the other, and nobody cared.

Down Ross Lake. Down the Skagit. Out onto the flats in the rain on the fifth afternoon, and the pumpkin field was mud now, rows of black dirt with the vines plowed under, and the hand-painted U-PICK sign was gone. Mount Baker was a gray shape behind gray cloud.

She was halfway across the field when she heard it.

Faint. Grainy. A voice at the bottom of a stairwell.

*— okay, but if Emmett's counting cards I'm gonna — *

She stopped dead in the mud.

She'd known it was coming. She'd been waiting for it for a hundred miles, the whole way down the Skagit, the way you wait for a step in the dark. She'd built the room in her head the night before and checked it twice, and checked the door, and checked the moose. It didn't matter. When it came, it hit her like the inlet had in September, cold and all at once, and every hair on her body went up.

The voice stopped mid-thought.

*...Leah?*

She put everything in the room. Turnagain, the café, the mine, the coat, the tree, the boat, the dock. Him. She shut the door. She stood the moose in front of it.

*Hey, kid.*

*LEAH!*

It came through so loud it hurt. And behind it, all at once, like lights coming on in a house, the others — Quil, startled, from somewhere along the beach at La Push; Embry, half asleep; and Jacob, from the riverbank at the Cullens' house, where he'd been lying in the wet ferns with a toddler curled against his side, watching the rain. She felt him come up out of it and turn north like a dog hearing its name.

*Leah.* Jacob, slow. *Hey.*

*Hey, Jake.*

*Where are you?*

She showed them. The mud. The rain. The mountain behind its cloud. She felt Seth's joy go through all of them like a kid running through a sprinkler, and Quil laughing, and Embry going *finally, God, he's been insufferable,* and under all of it Jacob, quiet, looking.

He'd found the door. Of course he had. He was the Alpha. Everything in their heads ran through his.

*What's that?*

*Canada.*

A pause. *There's a moose in front of it.*

*It's Canada's moose.*

She felt Seth and Quil and Embry all lean in toward it, curious, the way you'd lean toward a locked door in a house you thought you knew. She felt them bump up against it. She felt it hold.

She stood in the mud with the rain running off her fur and waited for Jacob to decide what to do about it.

She felt him think about it. She felt him think about Sam, and about what Sam would have done. She felt him decide.

*Okay,* Jacob thought. *It's yours.* And then, firmly, to all of them, with a little of the Alpha weight behind it, just enough to be felt: *Leave it alone. That's hers.*

And they did.

She stood there a moment longer. Then she started running again, south, toward the inlet and the lighthouse and the peninsula, and she let them come along with her in her head. She'd forgotten how loud it was. Quil was thinking about Claire's preschool Halloween costume, which had been a bumblebee. Embry was hungry. Seth was so happy he couldn't think in straight lines.

It was loud. It was crowded. It was a lot.

Behind the moose, in the room, the door stayed shut, and it was quiet in there.

*I said I wasn't doing that,* Jacob thought, as she hit the water at Point Wilson in the dark. Quietly. Just to her, as much as anything in there could be just to anyone. *Ever. I meant it.*

*I know you did,* she thought back, and swam.

---

Her mother was on the porch.

It was two in the morning when Leah came up through the trees behind the woodpile, and the porch light was on, and Sue Clearwater was sitting on the top step in her bathrobe and her husband's old fishing jacket with a mug of coffee going cold in her hands. She'd been sitting there, Leah learned later, since Seth had come running in from patrol at midnight yelling that Leah was in the water off Port Townsend and would be home by two.

Leah phased behind the woodpile. She untied the cord. She shook out the jeans and the flannel and the hoodie and the flattened pie box, and dressed, and put the mirror and the nine-dollar knife in her pocket, and walked out across the wet grass into the porch light.

Her mother stood up. The coffee went over on the steps.

Neither of them said anything at all.

Then Seth came out the screen door behind their mother at a dead run, six foot something and fifteen years old, and picked Leah up off the ground in the middle of the yard and swung her around like a sack of flour, and Leah, who had decided a long time ago that she was done crying in public, decided it didn't count in her own yard.


---

## VI.

The bloodsuckers' house still smelled like a hospital someone had soaked in perfume and set on fire.

She'd forgotten. That was the stupid part. Two months in Canada with woodsmoke and lake water and Dot's grill and spruce and snow, and she'd honestly forgotten, and on the third afternoon home, when she followed Seth up the long gravel drive through the cedars and the wind came off the river and hit her in the face with bleach and burnt sugar and something sweet rotting in the sun, her lip came up off her teeth before she could stop it.

"Oh, good," said Rosalie Cullen, from the white couch, when Leah came in the glass door behind Seth. "It's back."

"Missed you too, Blondie."

The child was in Rosalie's lap. Leah had seen her in Seth's head and in Jacob's for three days, but it was different seeing her with her own eyes. She'd been a lump in Bella's stomach when Leah left. Now she was sitting up on her own with a picture book open across her knees, and she looked like she was a year and a half, maybe two, with a head of bronze ringlets and Bella's big brown eyes, and she had a heartbeat, quick and light as a bird's. She smelled like the house and like a person both at once, and Leah's wolf didn't know what to do with it and lay down flat with its ears back.

Renesmee looked up from the book. She looked at Leah for a long moment with an expression that was much older than her face.

Then she held out one hand.

"Let her, Lee," said Jacob, from where he was sprawled on the floor by the fireplace. "She doesn't bite. Much."

Leah thought about saying no. She crouched down in front of the couch instead, with Rosalie watching her like a hawk watches a rabbit, and let the kid put her small hot hand flat on her cheek.

The picture came into her head all at once, the way the pack did. A gray wolf on a gray beach, running so fast the sand came up behind it in a spray, seen from far off, small and silver against the water. It was Seth's memory. She could feel Seth in it, how proud he was. And over the memory, like a voice laid over a song, something that was nothing but the child — a question, bright and curious and wordless.

Renesmee took her hand away.

"Where did you go?" she said, out loud, in a clear little voice.

"Canada."

"What's in Canada?"

Leah thought about it.

"Pie," she said.

Renesmee laughed. It was a real kid's laugh, high and surprised. Rosalie looked at Leah over the top of her head with an expression Leah couldn't read and didn't want to.

Edward caught her at the door on her way out.

He didn't touch her. None of them ever touched her. He stood a careful six feet away in the hall with his hands in his pockets and his strange gold eyes on her face, and he looked troubled in a way she'd only ever seen him look about Bella.

"You learned something in Canada," he said.

"I learned how to wash a sheet pan."

"There's a part of your mind I can't hear anymore." He said it slowly, like he was still turning it over himself. "The rest of you is as loud as ever. But there's a — room. And I can't get in." He tilted his head. "The only other mind I've ever met like that is my wife's."

"Good," Leah said.

"Who taught you that?"

She thought about the moose. Hard. In detail. Its legs, its lake, its sunset.

Edward winced. "Is that a *moose?*"

"Bye, Edward," said Leah, and went out onto the porch.

---

After that it was the same old thing.

She ran patrol with four voices in her head instead of none. Embry was always hungry. Quil was always thinking about Claire. Seth was always happy. Jacob was always at the bloodsuckers' house, and so was the kid, and so most of the time was everybody else, so Leah spent four afternoons a week sitting on the Cullens' porch steps in the rain, because she wasn't going in there again and she wasn't going to let Seth go in there without her either. Esme brought her sandwiches out on a plate. She ate them. She didn't say thank you, and Esme didn't seem to expect her to, and after two weeks of that they had some kind of understanding.

Her mother went to Charlie Swan's every night with a casserole, and some nights didn't come back until morning, and they both pretended that was normal. Charlie came to the Cullens' for dinner on Sundays and sat at the end of the long table and didn't ask a single question about anything he saw, and Leah, who'd known him her whole life as Dad's fishing buddy, found she respected him for it more than she'd ever respected him for anything. Emmett lost seventy-three dollars to Seth at poker. Bella's eyes went from red to orange to something close to the same strange gold as the rest of them. The kid grew. You could see it from week to week. By the start of December she looked closer to three than two and talked like she was ten and was reading Esme's books out loud on the white couch.

And every night, in her old room in the little house on the edge of the rez, with the rain on the window and her father's picture on the wall, Leah took the mirror out of the nightstand drawer and held it.

She'd look at her own face in the glass. Some nights she'd open her mouth.

Then she'd put it back in the drawer.

*I want to see if it holds.*

It held. Every day, it held. She'd go to the bloodsuckers' house and sit on the steps, and run patrol with the pack in her head, and come home and eat dinner with Seth and fall asleep in her own bed, and the door stayed shut, and the moose stood in front of it, and the room behind it stayed quiet and stayed hers.

It held for four weeks and two days.


---

## VII.

Edward heard him first. He usually did.

It was a Saturday afternoon in December, raining the way it had rained every day since Thanksgiving, and Edward was at the piano with his daughter on the bench beside him, letting her pick out the melody of Bella's lullaby with one finger while he played the left hand under it. The house was full of its ordinary noise. Esme in the kitchen. Emmett and Seth losing and winning at cards, respectively, at the dining table. Jacob on the floor by the fire, half asleep, his mind a slow warm hum of *Nessie, Nessie, Nessie.* Bella on the arm of the couch, watching the two of them at the piano, her thoughts silent to him as always. And out on the porch steps, the steady, closed, faintly hostile quiet of Leah Clearwater working her way through a plate of Esme's grilled-cheese sandwiches.

Then, at the bottom of the drive, a quarter mile away, a heartbeat.

It hadn't been there a second before. No car had come down the road from the highway; he'd have heard it at two miles. There'd been nothing, and now there was a heart beating at the edge of the cedars where the drive met the county road, slow and heavy and very strong, more like a large animal's than a man's. Then footsteps on the gravel. Boots. Unhurried. A man walking up a driveway.

Edward reached for the mind attached to it, the way he reached for every mind, without thinking.

There was nothing there.

He took his hand off the keys. Renesmee looked up at him.

Silence. The same silence he'd met only twice in his life: in his wife's head, and, these last four weeks, behind the door in Leah Clearwater's.

"Alice," he said, very quietly.

She was already at the top of the stairs. She came down them in the half-second it took him to say her name, and her face was pale even for her, and her eyes were wide and blank, and in her mind there was nothing either. No vision. No future. A blur where a future should have been, like the blur that Jacob and the pack made, only worse — a hole.

"I didn't see him," Alice said. "I don't see him *now.* Edward, there's nothing there."

Jasper was at the glass wall before she'd finished, his whole mind gone cold and tactical. Emmett had put his cards down. Carlisle was coming out of the study. Bella was off the couch with Renesmee already in her arms, though Edward hadn't seen her move. Jacob was up on his feet with his head up and his nostrils working, and his mind had gone from a hum to a single sharp spike: *Nessie.*

On the porch, Leah Clearwater's mind, which had been shut tight as a fist for four weeks, with that absurd badly painted moose standing guard in front of the one door he couldn't open, suddenly blew wide open like a window thrown up in a gale.

*Oh,* Leah thought. *Oh, you son of a* bitch.

---

She smelled him at four hundred yards, and for about two seconds she honestly thought she'd lost her mind.

The wind came up the drive off the river and through the cedars, wet, full of the bloodsuckers and the rain and the moss and the road. And under all of it, faint and then not faint, so out of place in this place that her brain refused to take it:

Earth after rain. Pine. Cold wet stone.

The struck match.

The sandwich stopped halfway to her mouth. Behind the door in her head, the moose fell over.

She heard, from inside the house, the piano stop. She heard Edward say *Alice.* She shoved the door back shut so hard it gave her a headache, and stood the moose back up in front of it, and knew she was too late, and didn't care. She put the sandwich down on Esme's plate. She stood up on the top step.

He came around the last bend in the drive by the big cedar.

He was in the long charcoal coat with the silver clasps down the front and the collar turned up, the one from the mine, the one from the dock. Black gloves. Black boots. A flat leather folder tucked under one arm. The rain was coming down hard enough to bounce off the gravel, and none of it was landing on him; it was stopping an inch above his hair and the shoulders of his coat and running off sideways, like he was walking under an umbrella nobody could see. He was walking right up the middle of the drive, at an ordinary pace, looking at the house. At the glass. At the size of it. Like a man who'd come to read a meter and found it was attached to a cathedral.

Then he looked at the porch, and stopped.

He stood in the middle of the drive in the rain, forty yards away. His head tipped to one side, a good thirty degrees, the way a dog's does when it hears a noise it can't place. She watched his nostrils flare. Once. Twice.

"Shit, girl," said Harry Potter. "Didn't know you were going to be here."

Leah stood on the top step with her hands shoved in her hoodie pocket and her heart going like a drum.

"I live here," she said.

He looked at the house. He looked at the glass wall and the white couch behind it and the faces that were, she knew without turning around, lined up behind it watching him. He looked back at her with an expression of deep concern.

"You live *here?*"

"Not *here* here. Down the road. On the rez."

"Ah." He seemed relieved. "You never said."

"You never asked."

"You said *home.*"

"This is home." She jerked her chin at the trees, the rain, the moss, the gray sky sitting on top of the cedars like a lid. "Olympic Peninsula. Welcome to Washington. It's wet."

"I'd noticed." He started walking again. Slower now. His eyes didn't leave her face. "You never rang."

"You said wait for me to ask."

"I said I'd wait. I didn't say I'd like it." He stopped at the foot of the porch steps, so they were nearly eye to eye, her on the top step and him on the gravel, with the rain sliding off the air over his head. Up close, she could see he looked tired. There was a line between his eyebrows that hadn't been there in Turnagain. "I didn't come to find you. For the record. I want that noted somewhere. I'm here for work." He lifted the leather folder an inch. "I'd no idea you'd be sat on the steps."

"You've got the stupid coat on."

"It's my work coat."

"It's stupid."

"You kissed me in it," Harry said.

Behind her, inside the house, through a wall of solid glass, every single vampire in it heard that, and she knew it, and she felt her face go hot from her collar to her hairline.

From somewhere inside, very clearly, Emmett Cullen said, "*Oh,* this is the best day of my *life* — "

Somebody hit him. It sounded like Rosalie.

The glass door slid open behind her. Carlisle Cullen came out onto the porch first, the way he always did, calm and blond and careful, with his hands where everyone could see them. Esme behind him. Edward and Bella, with the kid on Bella's hip and her bronze ringlets bouncing. Jasper, who came out last and stood at the corner of the porch where he could see everything, and didn't take his eyes off the man on the gravel for one second. And from the other end of the porch, where he'd come around the side of the house at a run, Jacob, already shaking down his arms, already halfway there.

"Jake," said Leah, without looking. "Don't."

Jacob stopped. He was breathing hard through his nose. He didn't phase.

Harry hadn't moved. He looked at Jacob, the size of him and the shaking, and at Jasper on the corner, and at Rosalie, who'd just come out the door with a look on her face like she was deciding which part of him to start with. Then he looked at Carlisle, and something in his face changed. The joke went out of it. It went flat and plain, the way it had gone on the mountain when he'd held up Kell's ring.

"Dr. Cullen," he said. "Afternoon. Sorry to turn up unannounced. Harry Potter. International Confederation of Wizards."

"Wizards," Emmett said, from the doorway, delighted.

"Yes," said Harry, without looking at him.

Carlisle didn't so much as blink. "And what can we do for you, Mr. Potter?"

Harry looked past him. At Bella. At the child on Bella's hip, who was looking back at him with her big brown eyes, and who reached out one small hand toward him through the air, curious, the way she'd reached for Leah.

"I'm here about your granddaughter," he said.

The porch went silent. Jacob made a sound that wasn't human.

"A child born in September, who looks three in December," Harry said, in the same plain voice. "In a town of three thousand people who've never seen magic in their lives and aren't supposed to. With a grandfather who's the chief of police, and who stood in his own garden this autumn and watched a lad turn into a wolf." He glanced at Jacob, briefly. "You, I'd guess."

Jacob's jaw worked. "Yeah."

"That sort of thing makes a noise, where I come from. It took a while to get to Geneva. It's got there." Harry tucked the leather folder back under his arm. "I've been sent to assess the breach, and take appropriate action if necessary. That's the wording. I didn't write it."

"Appropriate *action,*" said Rosalie, very softly, and Leah heard the hiss under it, and felt every hair on her arms go up.

"You touch her," Jacob said, low, shaking, "you so much as *look* at her — "

"Nobody's touching anybody." Harry hadn't raised his voice. He looked at Carlisle. "What I'd like, Dr. Cullen, is a cup of tea and a long talk. If you've got either."

Carlisle looked at him for a long moment. Then he glanced, very slightly, at Leah. At the girl who wouldn't set foot in his house, standing on his top step with her face still red, between the man in the gray coat and the rest of them.

"We can manage both," Carlisle said.

"Lovely." Harry put his foot on the bottom step. Then he stopped and looked up at Leah, and the joke came back into his face, just at the corners, the way it had on the dock in the snow. The corner of his mouth went up. She saw the canine.

"Also," he said, "and I appreciate the timing's not ideal — "

"Yes," said Leah.

He hadn't asked her anything yet. He grinned anyway.

Then she turned around, and walked in through the open glass door of the Cullens' house, past Carlisle and Esme and Edward and a gaping Emmett, into the stink of bleach and burnt sugar, on her own two feet, because she wanted to. That was a first. She heard him come up the steps behind her.
