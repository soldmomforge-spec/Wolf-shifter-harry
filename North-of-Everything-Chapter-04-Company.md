# North of Everything

## Chapter Four: Company

---

The cold came in first.

It came around the edges of the door the way it always had, damp and heavy, smelling of cedar and salt and wet gravel, and it brought the wolves in with it. The smell hit Leah at the back of the throat. Musk and wet fur and that hot, sharp, struck-match stink of bodies running a hundred and eight degrees. Close. All around the house. Under the alders, behind the woodshed, out along the road. Somewhere in the trees to the left, low and steady as an idling truck, somebody was growling.

Harry stepped out onto the porch.

Something went past Leah's ear.

She jerked her head back. It was a piece of fry bread. It came floating down the hall from the kitchen at about shoulder height, golden and puffed up and still steaming a little at the edges, swung neatly around her head, and drifted out the open door into Harry's waiting forepaw.

Leah looked at the fry bread. She looked at him.

"Seriously?"

"Your mum said they'd go to waste," Harry said, very low, and took a bite.

She followed him out and pulled the door shut behind her.

The porch was eight feet deep and maybe fifteen wide, with the old swing at the far end that nobody had sat in since Dad, and the Christmas lights strung along the rail, the big old-fashioned bulbs, red and green and blue and orange. Rain came down past the edge of the roof in silver strings. It drummed on the roof of the Subaru. It ran in sheets off the black hood of the Rolls-Royce. The gravel of the drive had gone dark and shiny, and at the bottom of the porch steps there was a puddle the size of a kiddie pool, the same puddle that had been there every winter of her life, because Dad had always said he'd fill that low spot in with gravel and never had.

Sam and Jacob had stopped at the far edge of the puddle.

Under the porch lights, she could see them properly.

Jacob had cut his hair. She'd known that from the window, but seeing it up close was different. He'd worn it long his whole life, and now it was short and ragged like the rest of them, and it made his face look older and leaner, all cheekbone and jaw. There was a new scar on his left shoulder, pale and puckered, a long curved line like something had tried to take a bite out of him and changed its mind. Rain ran down his bare chest. He had his arms loose at his sides and his weight back on his heels, and he was staring at Harry with his mouth a little bit open.

Sam was staring at her.

Leah made herself look back.

He looked the same. That was what got her, for a second. Same face. Same square jaw and heavy brows and long straight nose, and the mouth that had been the first mouth she ever kissed, at a bonfire on First Beach when she was sixteen. Same way of standing, shoulders back, chin down, feet planted, like he'd put roots down into the gravel. He was twenty-two now. He looked older than that. There were lines at the corners of his eyes she didn't remember. He held his shoulders like a man halfway up a long flight of stairs with something heavy on his back.

She waited for it to hurt.

She'd thought about this. On the plane, somewhere over Greenland, and before that, on and off, for fifteen months. What it would be like to look at him again. She'd figured it might feel like getting hit by a car. She'd figured it might feel like falling.

It felt like looking at a guy she used to know.

"What the hell," Jacob said, "are you?"

He was looking at Harry. They were all looking at Harry. She could feel the eyes in the trees, a dozen of them, more, low glints under the alders where the porch light reached.

Harry's left ear flicked.

"Glamour's up," he said to Leah, out of the side of his muzzle. "Full strength."

Leah looked at Jacob's face. At Sam's.

"Doesn't look like it's working," she said.

"Huh," said Harry. He took another bite of fry bread and chewed, thinking it over. "All of them, then."

"Leah," said Sam.

Just her name. The way he used to say it. Low and level, a little rough at the edges.

For a year that voice had lived inside her skull. It had been the last thing she heard before she fell asleep and the first thing she heard when she woke up. It had been under every thought she had—Sam worrying, Sam ordering, Sam feeling guilty, Sam thinking about Emily. When she'd torn herself out of the pack, the quiet afterward had been so big she'd sat down in the mud behind his woodshed and cried into her paws.

Now it was coming from fifteen feet away, out of a man standing barefoot at the edge of a puddle, and it sounded like a voice. Just a man's voice, in the rain.

"Sam," she said.

"Are you all right?"

"I'm fine."

His eyes went to Harry and stayed there. They went up, and up, from the heavy black boots to the long black coat to the ears. A muscle moved in his jaw.

"Step away from it, Leah," he said.

There was a short pause.

Harry stopped chewing. He turned his head and looked down at her. Both ears had tipped forward, in an expression she knew very well from fifteen months of breakfasts. It was the face he made when somebody at the next table in a diner said something so stupid he needed a second to make sure he'd heard it right.

"Did he just call me *it*?" he said.

"Yeah," said Leah.

"Right," said Harry.

He looked back out at the yard.

And something happened to him.

Leah would replay it later, in bed, for weeks, and laugh every time. He barely moved. He'd been leaning one shoulder on the porch post, loose, the way he leaned on everything, and he took his shoulder off the post and drew himself up to his full height, all six foot seven of him, until the tips of his ears nearly brushed the porch roof. He lifted his chin about an inch. He tugged the cuffs of his coat straight, first the left, then the right, two neat little tweaks of his claws. He brushed a crumb of fry bread off his lapel. His tail, which had been swaying slowly behind him, went completely still.

Then he looked down the length of his muzzle at Sam Uley the way you'd look at something a dog had dragged in and left on the kitchen floor.

"Good *evening*," said Harry.

Leah nearly fell off the porch.

It was his voice. The same low, rough rumble she'd been hearing every day for fifteen months, in the kitchen, in bed, in the dark. But everything on top of it had changed. The flat South African vowels were gone. The clipped ends were gone. What came out of his mouth instead was round and plummy and drawling, every vowel stretched long and polished until it shone, every consonant set down precisely in its place, like a man laying out silverware for a dinner party. It was the voice of a guy in a costume drama on PBS. It was the voice of a guy who owned a horse that had its own lawyer.

"You'll be the chap about the gutter," Harry said.

Sam blinked.

"The gutter," Harry repeated, patiently, and gestured with the fry bread toward the corner of the house, where the gutter on the left side sagged the way it always had. "Mrs. Clearwater did mention. Do go round the side, there's a good fellow. I believe there's a ladder by the woodshed." He considered Sam's bare chest, his cutoffs, his bare wet feet sunk in the gravel. "Though one does feel that for a job of that sort, you'd want shoes."

"I'm here about—" Sam started.

"Oh, *dear*." Harry took a delicate bite of fry bread. "Then I'm afraid I can't think what you're for."

Leah made a noise. She turned it into a cough.

Jacob made a noise too. His came out through his nose.

Sam's jaw had gone tight. Leah knew that jaw. She'd watched it go tight a hundred times through the pack mind, across Emily's kitchen table, when Paul was being Paul.

"My name is Sam Uley," Sam said. "This is Quileute land. You're on the reservation. And whatever you are, you don't belong here."

"Oh, I couldn't agree more," said Harry. "I belong in Devon, preferably in front of a fire, with a dog on my feet and a large brandy. But I'm a guest. Mrs. Clearwater invited me in." His ears tipped, very slightly. "That's how it works with your lot, isn't it? One has to be invited?"

There was a short, stunned silence.

"That's *vampires*," said Jacob.

"Is it?" Harry looked mildly surprised. "Oh. I do get you all muddled."

Somewhere in the trees, the growling went up half an octave. Leah bit the inside of her cheek so hard she tasted blood. Getting mixed up with the leeches was the worst thing you could say to any one of them. She'd felt Paul nearly phase over less, in the old days, through the pack mind—some tourist at First Beach calling him a *dog*, or the Cullens' blond one wrinkling her nose.

"What are you?" said Sam.

"English," said Harry. "Mostly. On my father's side."

"You know what I mean."

"I'm afraid I very rarely do, old chap. It's been remarked upon."

Jacob had put one hand over the bottom half of his face. His shoulders were moving. Leah caught his eye across the puddle, and his eyebrows went up into his ragged hair—*is he for real?*—and she gave him the smallest shrug in the world, and Jacob turned around and looked at the Subaru for a while.

When he turned back his face was mostly under control.

"Is Seth okay?" he said.

It came out rougher than the rest. Leah remembered, with a little jolt, that Seth was his. Seth was Jacob's the way she'd been Sam's, once. Jacob had felt Seth come tearing out of the woods an hour ago with his whole head full of *Leah Leah Leah*, and then felt him phase back in the yard and go quiet.

Harry turned his head and regarded Jacob properly for the first time. His nostrils flared, once.

"Seth," he said, "is in the kitchen, eating his own weight in fry bread and weeping. He's perfectly well."

"*Weeping?*"

"Copiously." Harry tilted his head. "You'll be Jacob."

Jacob hesitated. "Yeah."

"Seth's mentioned you. You smell of motor oil."

"Uh," said Jacob. "Thanks?"

Harry inclined his head, graciously, as if Jacob had said something rather sweet.

Sam took a step forward. His bare foot went into the edge of the puddle and he didn't seem to notice.

"Look," he said. "I don't know what you are, or where you came from, or what you want with her. But you need to understand what's been going on here. We've had leeches coming through since January. Every few weeks. Nomads. People have died. Kids are phasing—twelve-year-olds, thirteen-year-olds. Dylan Littlesea went through the wall of his classroom last week." He was looking at Leah now, and some of the Alpha had gone out of his face, and what was left looked tired. "And there's something else out there since October. North of the lake. Something we can't catch. And now you show up. Something none of us has ever smelled before. On our land. With—"

His eyes went to the car.

"In a Rolls-Royce," Harry agreed. "Yes. I know. Dreadfully vulgar. I did ask for something more discreet."

"Nice car," said somebody from the drive.

Leah turned her head.

She hadn't heard him come out of the trees. She'd been too busy not laughing. But there he was, sauntering up the gravel in a pair of cutoff sweatpants and nothing else, soaked to the skin and steaming in the cold, with his hair buzzed down to stubble and a smirk on his face that he'd been wearing, as far as Leah could remember, since the sixth grade.

Paul Lahote.

He walked right past the puddle, and past Sam and Jacob, and around the back of the Subaru. He stopped beside the Rolls-Royce. He looked it over, slowly, from the little silver woman on the hood to the tinted back windows. Then he turned around and leaned on it. He leaned back against the rear door with his arms folded across his chest and his ankles crossed, the whole wet muddy length of him pressed against the black paint, and looked up at the porch, and grinned.

Harry's eyes moved to Paul. They took their time getting there.

He looked at Paul for a long moment. At the arms. At the ankles. At the mud on Paul's calves, and the wet sand stuck to his back, and the place where that back met the door.

"I say," said Harry. "Would you mind terribly getting *orf* the motorcar? Gerald only washed it this morning, and I'd really rather not have poor-people dirt on it." He paused. "Thank you *ever* so much."

Leah turned around and faced the front door.

She put her forehead against it. The wood was cold and wet and smelled like twenty years of paint. Her shoulders were shaking. She pressed her lips together so hard they hurt.

*Gerald.*

There was no Gerald. There had never been a Gerald. That car had spent the last month under a dust sheet in a carriage house on Dartmoor, and the only living thing that had been near it was a two-hundred-year-old house-elf in a tea towel, and his name was not Gerald.

*Poor-people dirt.*

From a guy who'd spent the first eleven years of his life sleeping in a cupboard under the stairs.

"Who," said Paul, slowly, in a voice gone flat and ugly, "the *fuck* do you think you are?"

Leah turned back around just in time to see Harry blink.

He blinked like the question had honestly surprised him. Like somebody had asked him what color the sky was. His ears came up, both of them, all the way, in mild polite astonishment, and he lowered the fry bread and looked at Paul with something close to pity.

"Well," he said, "I'm Sir Harry Potter-Black, of course."

The rain went on.

Nobody said anything. Out in the trees, very distinctly, one of the wolves sneezed.

Jacob made a sound like a tire going flat and turned around and looked at the Subaru again.

"*Sir*," said Paul.

"Sir," Harry agreed.

"Like a *knight*."

"Well. Technically it's *Lord*." Harry sighed, a small, put-upon sigh. "Twice, as it happens, which I'm aware is terribly greedy of me. But one doesn't like to make a fuss." He took another bite of fry bread, chewed, and swallowed. "Sir Harry James Potter-Black. Lord of Ashford Water. Order of Merlin, First Class. Honorary President of the Tamarack Crossing Pie Appreciation Society, a post I take *extremely* seriously." He dabbed at the corner of his muzzle with one claw. "Welcome in eleven countries. Twelve, if you count Liechtenstein."

Leah laughed out loud.

She couldn't help it. It came out of her in a bark, sharp and loud, and she slapped a hand over her mouth, but it was too late. She'd heard him say that once before. On a ridge above a black lake, standing over a blood mage in a ring of stones, with a plastic pie container set down neatly on top of an altar. *Eleven countries signed it. Twelve, if you count Liechtenstein.*

Harry didn't look at her. His tail flicked, once, at the very tip.

"Nobody counts Liechtenstein," he added, to Paul, kindly.

"Potter-*Black*?" said somebody else. Leah didn't see who. One of the ones in the trees, phased back, standing just inside the alders—Jared, maybe, from the voice. "Like—Black? Like *Jake*?"

Jacob had stopped looking at the Subaru. He'd gone still.

Leah's stomach turned over once, quick and cold. She saw the mailbox again. Hand-painted, flaking. *BLACK.* A little red house with a ramp.

She shut it.

"I very much doubt it, dear boy," said Harry, without looking toward the trees. "The London Blacks. Dreadful house in London. Full of doxies and bad portraits. Nothing to do with anyone here, I'm sure." His ear flicked toward the alders. "Do come out, by the way, if you're going to talk. It's rude to shout from the shrubbery."

Nobody came out of the shrubbery.

"I don't give a shit," said Paul, "if you're the fucking Queen of England."

"Oh, I've met her," said Harry. "Lovely woman. Fearsome at cards."

Paul stared at him.

Leah was going to ask about that later. She was going to ask about that later at *length*.

Harry sighed again and turned his head toward Sam, with the air of a man who's had enough of dealing with the staff and would like to speak to a manager.

"Samuel," he said. "Is that one yours?"

Sam's jaw worked.

"Only he's *leaning*," said Harry.

For a second Leah honestly thought Sam was going to let it go. She saw him think about it. She knew how Sam thought, she'd been inside it for a year—he'd let Paul off the leash a hundred times, when Paul's temper was useful, when somebody needed to be scared. She saw him think about letting Paul lean on that car all night just to make a point.

Then Sam glanced up at her. Just for a second.

"Paul," he said, between his teeth. "Off the car."

Paul held it for a beat, to show he could. Then he pushed off the door with his shoulders and stepped away and stood there in the gravel with his arms still folded, glaring.

He'd left a mark. A long wet smear of mud and sand down the black paint, from about the height of his shoulder blades to his knees, with the shape of his shoulders at the top of it like the print of a dirty angel.

Harry looked at it.

He didn't do anything Leah could see. But as she watched, the mud began to move. It slid down the door all at once, the whole smear of it, like the paint had decided it didn't want it, and gathered itself into a thin brown stream that ran off the bottom of the door and dripped onto the gravel. The door was clean. It gleamed under the porch lights, black and wet and perfect, with the Christmas bulbs reflected in it.

Paul looked down at the gravel by his feet, where the mud had landed. Then he looked at the door. Then he took a step away from the car.

"Thank you ever so much," said Harry.

Out in the dark, more of them were coming out of the trees now. Leah watched them. Some in fur and some on two legs. Jared, on two legs, his broad face set hard. Quil, in fur, chocolate brown, standing on Jacob's side of the yard instead of Sam's—which was news. Embry beside him, gray with the dark spots along his back. A huge dun-colored wolf she didn't know at all. A small sandy-colored one, gangly, all legs, who couldn't have been older than thirteen and kept glancing back over its shoulder at the trees like it wanted to go home. More behind them. Fur and eyes and wet breath smoking in the cold. They didn't come any closer than the edge of the light. They stood there in a long ragged half circle around the yard, at the edge of the dark, and watched.

Harry looked them over, unhurried, from one end of the line to the other.

"Is this some sort of club?" he asked Sam.

"What?"

"A club. A society. Only you've all got the same haircut." Harry gestured at Jared with the last of the fry bread. "And none of you appear to own a shirt. In *December*. On the *Pacific coast*." His ears tipped forward with interest. "Is there a charity calendar?"

"There's not a *calendar*," said Leah, from behind her hand.

"There ought to be. It'd sell terribly well." Harry looked Sam up and down. "December could be you, Samuel. Standing in a puddle. Looking stern."

Jacob lost it.

He'd been holding on, Leah could tell, for a while now, with his jaw clenched and his face doing a lot of work. It went all at once. He laughed out loud, a big helpless honk of a laugh that went echoing off across the yard, and then he clapped both hands over his mouth and bent over at the waist and stayed there, wheezing, while Sam turned his head and looked at him.

"Sorry," Jacob said, muffled. "Sorry. Sorry, man. Allergies."

Paul was shaking.

Leah saw it start. She knew it. A fine fast tremor down his arms, his folded hands coming apart, his fingers flexing. His lip pulling back off his teeth. He'd always been the fastest of them to go, Paul—one word too many, one look, and the wolf came up through him like a wave over a seawall.

"Paul," said Sam sharply.

Too late.

Paul came apart in the middle of a step. One second he was a soaked, furious twenty-year-old in cutoff sweats, and the next there was a sound like ripping canvas and a spray of wet gravel and a wolf the size of a horse, dark silver-gray, coming at the porch.

He came fast. He'd always been fast. Three strides across the drive and he launched himself off the edge of the puddle, straight at the steps, straight at Harry, mouth open, ears flat—

—and stopped.

He stopped in the air. He hung there about six feet off the ground, halfway up the porch steps, with his legs still reaching and his jaws still open, exactly as if somebody had pressed pause. The rain went on falling all around him. It ran off his fur and dripped from his paws and his belly onto the steps below. His eyes rolled. His legs paddled, slowly, at nothing. The Christmas lights along the porch rail reflected red and green and blue in his eyes.

Harry hadn't moved. He was still looking at Sam. He finished the last bite of fry bread, chewed it thoroughly, and swallowed.

"Samuel," he said. "One of yours appears to be in my airspace."

Out in the yard, nobody breathed. The line of wolves at the edge of the light had gone rigid, every one of them, every ear flat and every hackle up. The small sandy one had sat down hard on its haunches.

Paul made a sound in his throat. High and thin. Leah had never in her life heard Paul Lahote make a sound like that.

Then he moved. Sideways, smoothly, still hanging in the air, like he was on a wire. He drifted back down the steps and out over the drive, and stopped, and hovered for one second directly over the middle of Dad's puddle.

And then he went down into it.

Harry set him down in it, gently, the way you'd set down a bag of groceries, so that he landed on all four feet in nine inches of cold brown water with a flat heavy *splat* that threw muddy water halfway to the Subaru. Then he stood there in the puddle, dripping, with his mouth open.

"Do mind the puddle," said Harry.

"*Paul*," said Sam. "*Enough.*"

Leah felt it.

She didn't feel it in her own head. She felt it through the air, somehow, the way you'd feel a thunderclap through your feet. Sam's voice had dropped all the way down into the thing it did, the thing she'd felt every day for a year, the thing that went past every part of you that got a vote and took hold of the parts that didn't. And Paul's body answered it. She watched it happen. His legs folded under him all at once and he went down flat in the puddle, belly in the water, chin in the water, ears pinned. A long thin whine came out of him. But his eyes—his eyes were still furious. His eyes were still fighting. His body was lying down in a mud puddle in front of nineteen people and his eyes wanted to kill somebody, and couldn't, because Sam had said so.

Leah had felt it from the inside. She'd never seen it from out here.

It turned her stomach over.

Without meaning to, she put her thumb against the inside of the heavy gold ring on her right hand, and pressed.

"Thank you, Samuel," said Harry. "I'd keep that one on a shorter lead, if I were you."

Sam didn't answer him. He was looking at her.

"Leah," he said. "Come down here. Please."

She didn't move.

"Leah." His voice changed. It went down. It was the voice he'd just used on Paul. It came across the yard at her like a hand reaching for the back of her neck. "*Come here.*"

Leah stood on her mother's porch and waited for it.

She waited for the hook. She waited for the yank behind her breastbone, the one she'd felt every day for a year, the one that used to bunch her legs under her before she'd even decided to move. She waited for even the ghost of it. The memory of it. A twinge.

Nothing came.

The ring sat on her finger, heavy and cool. The rain came down. Her mom's Christmas lights blinked red and green and blue and orange along the rail. Inside her head there was nobody but her.

*Nobody gets in,* Harry had said, in a round room at the top of a tower. *Not even me, Leah. Ever.*

"No," Leah said.

She watched Sam hear it.

She watched him realize he'd tried it on her, and what he'd tried, and that it hadn't worked—that he'd reached for the hook in front of everyone and found nothing there. Something went across his face, fast, and it looked a lot like shame.

"I believe the lady said no, Samuel," said Harry. "Do keep up."

Sam took a breath. Then he went still. His head came up a fraction, and his nostrils flared, and Leah realized with a cold drop in her stomach what he was doing. He was smelling her. Really smelling her, for the first time since she'd come out the door.

She knew what he'd find. Milk. Baby. Layla's smell was all over her—on her hands, on her shirt, in her hair, on the flannel she'd had her pressed against for eleven hours on a plane.

"There's a baby in that house," Sam said slowly.

"Gosh," said Harry. "You *are* perceptive."

"Leah." Sam's eyes had left Harry. They were on her and nowhere else. "Whose—"

"Mine," said Leah.

She let it sit there in the rain for a second.

"Mine and his. She's four weeks old. Her name's Layla."

The yard went very quiet.

She watched it go through all of them. Sam. Jacob, who'd straightened up from his allergies. Jared, at the tree line. The wolves behind him. She saw Embry's ears come up. She saw the little sandy one look from her to Harry and back with its mouth hanging open.

They all knew. That was the thing. Every single one of them who'd been in the pack mind with her that year knew exactly what she'd thought about herself, because it had been in her head every day, and her head had been theirs. They'd all heard her think it. *Dead end.* They'd all felt her not-cry about it in the dark.

In the puddle, Paul lifted his chin out of the water. He'd phased back. He was lying on his stomach in the mud, naked, in nine inches of water, with Sam's order still holding him flat, and his face was a mask of complete disbelief.

"Clearwater got *knocked up*?" he said. "I thought she was—"

He stopped.

"*Lady Potter-Black*," said Harry. His voice hadn't risen at all. If anything, it had gotten softer, and rounder, and more polite. "And I'd moderate the language, if I were you. There's an infant within earshot, and she's at a terribly impressionable age."

Paul stared up at him out of the mud.

"*Lady*?" he said.

"Do try to keep up," said Harry. "There'll be a quiz."

Leah was standing behind him, a little to one side, and so she was the only one who saw his tail. It had come unstuck. It was hanging down behind him under the hem of his coat, and the very tip of it was moving. Back and forth. Just slightly. Like a metronome.

Some of the fear went out of her at the sight of it.

Because she'd thought of it, standing there with all of them looking at her—she'd thought of it the second Sam said *baby*. Nineteen of them, now. Most of them hadn't imprinted. She thought of Quil at a bonfire, gazing across the sand at a toddler in a pink raincoat. She thought of Jacob in a glass house, looking down at a baby on the very first day of her life. And she'd thought, *Every one of those boys is going to want to see her.*

She was glad Layla was inside. She was so glad Layla was inside that her knees felt loose with it.

"You said you couldn't," Sam said quietly.

It was meant for her. It was the most private thing he'd said all night, and he'd said it in front of everybody.

"Yeah," said Leah. "I was wrong."

Sam looked at her for a long moment. Then he looked up at Harry. Then back at her. Something was going on in his face that she couldn't read anymore, and she realized she didn't need to.

"Emily's been worried about you," he said. "We both have. Since you left. She—"

"Emily?" said Harry.

He said it brightly, politely, with his head tilted a little to one side, the way you'd say the name of somebody you'd been introduced to at a party twenty minutes ago and immediately forgotten.

"Remind me," he said. "Your wife, is it?"

Sam stared at him.

"...Yes," he said.

"How *lovely* for you both," said Harry. "Do give her our best."

And Leah understood, all at once, what he was doing.

He knew exactly who Emily was. She'd told him. In a tent in Mongolia in February, in the dark, with the wind screaming across the steppe outside and the two of them wrapped up in four sleeping bags zipped together, she had told him about Sam Uley in about five sentences. First boyfriend. Three years. He disappeared for two weeks and came back different. Then her cousin came to visit. Then he married her cousin. She'd left out the why, because the why was the stupidest and most humiliating part. Harry had listened to all five sentences without saying a word. Then he'd put his forepaw on the back of her neck and left it there until she fell asleep.

He knew. And he was standing on her mother's porch taking the biggest, ugliest, most important thing that had ever happened to her, the thing that had eaten two years of her life and chased her a thousand miles north, and holding it up to the porch light, and turning it over, and saying, very politely, *Sorry—who?*

And Sam felt it. She watched him feel it. She watched him realize that to the giant wolf in the long black coat on Sue Clearwater's porch, Sam Uley and his wife and his whole tragic love story were about as interesting as the gutter.

Something in Leah's chest that had been clenched for two years let go, a little, like a fist uncurling.

"She was my responsibility," said Sam. His voice had gone hoarse. "Leah. For a year. She was my pack. I—"

"Was she?" said Harry. "How extraordinary." He turned his head and looked down at Leah, ears up, all polite inquiry. "Darling. Were you aware that you were his responsibility?"

"News to me," said Leah.

"News to her, Samuel."

From the far side of the puddle, Jacob made a small sound and put his face in his hands.

Harry reached into the inside pocket of his coat and took out the old gold pocket watch and flipped it open and looked at it.

Leah knew what he was looking at. Two tiny points of light behind the glass, pulsing. One big and slow, one small and quick. Her and Layla. Layla, fifteen feet behind them on the other side of the wall, in her grandmother's arms. Her small quick heartbeat going steady and even. Fine. She was fine.

To everybody in the yard, it looked like a man in a very expensive coat checking the time because he was bored.

"Gosh," said Harry. "Is that the hour?" He snapped the watch shut and put it away. "Was there anything else, Samuel? Only Mrs. Clearwater's doing something with a salmon, and I'd hate for it to spoil."

Sam stood in the edge of the puddle with the rain running off him.

He looked, for a moment, like he didn't know what to do with his hands. Leah had never seen Sam not know what to do with his hands. For a year she'd watched him walk into every room and every fight like he'd already decided how it was going to go.

"What do you want?" he said finally. To Harry. "Here. What do you actually want?"

"Tonight? Dinner," said Harry. "Then a hot bath, if Mrs. Clearwater's boiler is up to it, which I rather doubt. Then bed." He tilted his head. "And in the morning, I thought I'd take a stroll north of the lake and see what's been leaving that smell."

Sam's head came up.

"You don't know what it is."

"Rot and copper," said Harry, "under something sweet. Something in a bottle—cologne, or scent—laid on rather thick. It doesn't leave a trail where it doesn't want to. It's been on the north shore since October. You've had a dozen of your best boys on it for two months, and you haven't laid a paw on it." His voice hadn't changed at all. It was still round and lazy and drawling, every vowel still polished. "I've met one before, actually. Dreadful bore. Wouldn't stop talking." He brushed a raindrop off his sleeve. "I set him on fire."

Nobody said anything.

The rain drummed on the Subaru.

Leah watched Sam's face. She watched him look at Harry and, for the first time since he'd walked up the drive, actually see him. The size of him. The stillness. Paul, lying in the puddle where he'd been put down like a bag of groceries. The car door with no mud on it.

"If it's what you say it is," Sam said slowly, "it's on our land. It's our problem."

"Then by all means," said Harry, "do carry on not catching it. I'll be along in the morning."

Sam's mouth went thin. But he didn't argue. Leah saw him not argue. She saw him swallow it.

He looked back at her.

"Are you okay?" he said. Quieter. The Alpha was all gone out of it now. "Leah. Really. Are you—is he—"

Leah thought about how to answer that.

She could tell him about the dock under the peach-colored moon. The house that bowed when she walked through the door. The ring that kept her head her own. The bathroom floor in Atlin, and the claws he'd let her break, and the way his tail had been going since June like it had a battery in it.

"Sam," she said, "he bought a *plane* so nobody would cough on her."

Sam blinked. "He what?"

"I'm good," Leah said. "I'm really good."

He looked at her for a long time.

"Okay," he said. "Okay. I'm—" He stopped. His throat moved. "I'm glad."

"Go home, Sam," said Leah. "Tell Emily I said hi."

She meant it. That was the weird part. She heard it come out of her mouth and realized she meant it. Not much. A little. Enough.

Sam nodded once. He looked down at Paul in the puddle.

"Paul," he said. "Up."

Paul got up. Mud slid off him in sheets. He stood there naked and dripping in the porch light and glared up at Harry with murder in his eyes, and Harry looked back at him with an expression of mild, vague goodwill, the way you'd look at a neighbor's cat sitting on your fence.

Sam turned and started down the drive, and Paul went after him, and the line of wolves at the edge of the light began to break up and fade back into the trees.

"Do mind the gate on your way out, Samuel," Harry called after him.

Sam stopped. He looked back over his shoulder.

"There's no gate," he said.

"Then do mind the place where one *ought* to be," said Harry.

Sam looked at him for a second longer. Then he turned and walked off down the gravel into the dark, and the rain closed around him, and he was gone. Leah heard the crack and rip of him phasing somewhere past the alders, and the heavy thud of paws on wet earth going away fast, east, toward his house and Emily's. One by one the others went after him. The silver-gray one—Paul—went last, with a snarl over his shoulder at the porch that nobody answered.

Then there was only the rain.

Jacob was still standing at the edge of the puddle.

He'd taken his face out of his hands. He was looking up at the porch with an expression Leah couldn't quite name, somewhere between awe and a kid who'd just watched the best fight of his life from the front row.

"Okay," he said. "That was the greatest thing I've seen all year. And I watched an army of vampires back down in a snowfield."

Harry didn't answer him. He was still facing the drive with his chin up and his paws folded in front of him, his ears pointed down the road, listening. Ten seconds. Fifteen. Leah could almost see him following Sam's paws away through the trees, a mile out, more, until they were too far to matter.

Then he let out a long breath, and slumped back against the porch post so hard the whole porch roof creaked, and scrubbed both forepaws down his face.

"*Eish*," he said. "My face hurts."

Jacob's head jerked up.

"What happened to your *accent*?"

"Which one?" said Harry.

Jacob opened his mouth. Then he shut it. Then he laughed again, quieter this time, and shook his head, and looked at Leah.

"Seth's got patrol at six," he said. "Tell him I said take the night. Tell him—" He stopped. "Can I see her? The baby. Just for a second."

"No," said Leah.

It came out sharper than she meant it to. Faster. Like something snapping shut.

Jacob held up both hands. "Okay," he said. "Okay. Not tonight."

"Not *tonight*," Leah said, and heard what was under it, and didn't care.

Jacob looked at her for a second. She saw him figure something out. She didn't know what—maybe just that she'd been away a long time, and come home different, and there were things she wasn't going to explain to him standing in her mom's yard in the rain. He nodded slowly.

"Welcome home, Leah," he said. "Seriously. You look—" He hunted for the word. "You look good. Like, *happy*. I didn't know your face could do that."

"Screw you, Jake."

"There she is." He grinned, and backed off a step, and looked up at Harry. "I'll come by tomorrow. The Cullens are gonna find out about you, you know. Edward's gonna hear it out of somebody's head by morning."

"Then tell them to knock," said Harry.

Jacob studied him. "Polite knock or posh knock?"

"Depends how they ask," said Harry.

Jacob laughed one more time, and turned, and jogged off across the yard into the trees, and was gone. A second later Leah heard him phase, and a single long low howl went up out of the woods to the west, rising, and broke off, and then there was only the rain on the roof.

She stood there on the porch and listened to it.

"One stayed," said Harry, quietly.

"Where?"

"Behind the woodshed. Young. Brown." His nose worked. "He's trying very hard to be a woodpile."

"Of course Sam left somebody," said Leah. "Of course he did."

"Let him stay," said Harry. "He'll get cold."

"We run hot."

"He'll get bored, then." Harry pushed himself up off the porch post. He looked down at her. His ears had gone sideways, and there was something careful in his face that she knew meant he was checking. "You okay?"

Leah opened her mouth to answer, and what came out instead was a laugh.

She didn't mean it to. It just started. It came up out of her stomach the way it had on the ridge above Tamarack Crossing, with him standing in front of her in a Led Zeppelin T-shirt asking if she wanted a drink, and it would not stop. She laughed until she had to sit down on the top step of the porch in the wet. She laughed until her ribs hurt and her eyes ran and everything Healer Pye had warned her would be sore for a month was sore. She laughed with her face in her hands.

"*Gerald*," she gasped.

Harry sat down on the step beside her.

"*Poor-people dirt*," Leah wheezed. "*Sir Harry Potter-Black, of course*—"

"I panicked," said Harry.

"You did *not* panic. You've never panicked in your *life*—"

"He was *leaning*, Leah."

She fell over sideways against his shoulder and laughed into his coat until there was nothing left. He sat there in the rain on her dead father's porch steps and let her, with his tail curled around both their feet and going *thump, thump, thump* against the wet boards.

Behind them, the front door opened.

"Are you two coming in," said Sue Clearwater, "or are you going to sit out there in the wet all night like a couple of fools?"

Seth was lying on the living-room floor.

He was flat on his back on the braided rug in front of the TV, with his arms out and his knees up, staring at the ceiling and making a high, thin, wheezing sound that went on and on. There were tears running out of the corners of his eyes into his ears. When Leah came in the door he lifted his head off the rug, looked at her, looked at Harry ducking in behind her, and put his head back down and started wheezing again.

"He's been like that for five minutes," said Sue. "I think something's broken."

She had Layla in the crook of her arm, wrapped up in Kreacher's gray blanket, and she'd been standing at the front window. The curtain was pulled back, and the window was cracked open about two inches at the bottom, letting in the cold. Leah looked at the crack, and then at her mom.

"You heard all that."

"Every word," said Sue.

Harry was already crossing the room. He didn't say anything. He held out his forepaws, and Sue looked up at him for a second and then put the baby into them, and he lifted Layla up under his chin and put his nose down against the top of her head, between her folded ears, and breathed in. A long, slow breath. His eyes closed.

Layla woke up. She made a small cross grumble about the cold coming off his coat. Harry bent his head and licked her left ear, once, slow, from the base to the silver tip, and then the right one, and the grumble stopped, and from somewhere deep in her small chest came the chirrup. That low, rumbling, kettle-thinking-about-boiling sound.

Harry's ears came back up. His shoulders, which Leah realized had been up around his ears since the front door opened, came down about two inches.

Sue watched him do it. She didn't say anything about it.

"*Gerald*," said Seth, from the floor, in a strangled voice. "*Who is Gerald*."

"No idea," said Harry, into Layla's fur. He'd gone back to his own voice. "He came to me. Lovely chap. Bad knee. Served in the Falklands."

"*Is Gerald real?*"

"He is now," said Harry.

Seth rolled over onto his side and curled up and made the noise again.

Leah shrugged off her wet coat and hung it on the hook by the door, the same hook she'd hung her coat on every day of her life until she was nineteen, and sat down on the arm of the couch, because her legs still weren't quite working.

"Okay," she said. "I have questions."

"Ja, I figured."

"Where did that *voice* come from?"

Harry sat down on the floor with his back against the wall by the woodstove, because the couch would have broken under him and he knew it, and settled Layla against his chest. He thought about it.

"School," he said. "There was a boy in my year. Draco Malfoy. Seven years of him. His dad talked like that too. Lucius. Came up to the school every time Draco broke a fingernail, sweeping about in a cape." His ears flicked sideways. "You spend seven years with somebody saying *Potter* at you in that voice, you pick it up. I used to do Draco for Ron in the common room to make him laugh. I can do Lucius, too, but it's harder. You have to sneer through your nose."

"*Draco*," said Leah.

"His mum and dad were like that," said Harry. "He had a sad life, honestly. He's all right now. He married a nice girl. Sends me a card at Christmas."

"And the Queen? You've met the *Queen*?"

"Once. At a thing." Harry rubbed one claw gently along the top of Layla's head. "Kingsley took me. Minister for Magic, he's an old friend. She's supposed to know about us—the Prime Minister's told, and she's told, it's a whole arrangement. She looked at me for about five seconds through the glamour and said, 'Oh. You're one of *Kingsley's*.' And then she asked if I played bridge."

"Do you?"

"I do *now*," said Harry darkly. "She took eleven pounds off me."

Sue made a sound. Leah turned around. Her mother had her hand over her mouth and her eyes were very bright.

Harry looked up at Sue, and something went out of his face. His ears went down.

"Sue," he said. "The thing I said. About the dirt. On the car." He looked at the rug. "I'm sorry. That was for Paul. I shouldn't have said it on your porch."

Sue took her hand away from her mouth.

"I know who it was for," she said. "I've been called worse than that by a woman at the county assessor's office in Port Angeles, and she meant it." She folded her arms. "Paul Lahote has been leaning on things that don't belong to him since he was six years old. He leaned on Harry's truck at the Fourth of July picnic when he was eleven and put a dent in the fender, and never said a word. Harry found the dent with Paul's handprint still in it." Her mouth twitched. "He'd have liked the puddle."

Harry's ears came back up, slowly.

"Sir," said Sue. "You said *Sir*."

"That was a lie," Harry admitted. "I'm not a sir. I'm a lord. Two lords. It's a long story with a lot of paperwork in it." His ears went pink. "I never use it. I nearly turned Ron into a teapot once for calling me 'm'lord' in front of the Wizengamot."

"So 'Sir' was a *demotion*," said Leah.

"A big one," said Harry. "I was being very humble."

Seth sat up. He wiped his eyes on his bare forearm. His face was red and blotchy and he looked about nine years old.

"Do it again," he said. "Do the voice."

Harry looked at him. Then he lifted his chin about an inch, and his ears came up, and he looked down his muzzle at Seth on the rug.

"Seth, old bean," he said, round and plummy and drawling, "do put on a *shirt*. There are ladies present, and one of them is four weeks old."

Seth fell back over onto the rug.

"He's right," said Sue. "Go put on a shirt, Seth."

Her room was exactly the same.

Leah stood in the doorway and looked at it. She'd been braced for it to be different. A sewing room, maybe. Boxes. Mom's exercise bike. She'd been gone fifteen months, she'd left a note on a Thriftway receipt, she'd had no right to expect anything.

It was the same.

Same narrow bed against the wall, under the window, with the star quilt Grandma Clearwater had made for her when she was born—blue and white and red, a big eight-pointed star in the middle—pulled up tight and smooth. Same dresser with the drawer that stuck. Same Huskies pennant over the desk, purple and gold, that she'd bought at a game in Seattle when she was fifteen and made Dad promise she'd go there. A Mariners schedule from the 2006 season still thumbtacked to the closet door. Somebody had dusted. The window was cracked open an inch for the air, the way Mom always kept them.

On top of the dresser, face down, there was a little photo frame.

Leah didn't have to turn it over. She knew what it was. A photo-booth strip from the mall in Port Angeles, four pictures, her and Emily at fifteen, making faces. Emily with her tongue out. Leah with her eyes crossed. Both of them laughing so hard in the last picture they were blurry. She'd turned it face down the week Sam left her, and she hadn't been able to throw it out, and she hadn't been able to turn it back over, and there it still was.

She left it.

"Small," said Harry, behind her.

He was standing in the hall, with Layla on his chest, looking in at the narrow bed.

"It's a twin," said Leah. "I was a kid."

"Ja. I can see that." He ducked in under the doorframe and looked around, ears swiveling, taking it in. The pennant. The schedule. The face-down frame on the dresser, which his eyes went over and didn't stop on. He sat down on the floor at the end of the bed, his back against the wall, his long legs stretched out across most of the remaining floor. The room was so small his footpaws were nearly touching the closet door.

Layla had started rooting at his coat, snuffling, her small mouth working.

"She's hungry," said Harry.

"She's always hungry."

"She gets that from you."

"She gets that from *both* of us." Leah sat down on the edge of the bed and held out her arms, and he gave Layla to her, and she got her shirt out of the way and got Layla latched on, and Layla went quiet and fierce and serious the way she always did when she was eating, both tiny forepaws kneading at Leah's skin, her rice-grain claws prickling. Her little tail lay curled against Leah's arm, twitching at the very end.

For a while nobody said anything. The rain tapped on the window. Down the hall, Leah could hear her mother in the kitchen, the clank of a pot going onto the stove, the radio coming on low. Some country station out of Aberdeen.

"That was the best thing I've ever seen," Leah said.

Harry's ears went pink inside.

"I mean it. I have watched you walk through a blood mage's curses like they were rain. I've watched you set a man on fire. I've watched you pick a jeep up off a goblin with your *brain*. That was better."

"It was a bit much," said Harry.

"It was *so* much." She looked at him. "Why?"

He thought about it. He sat there at the end of her childhood bed with his ears half up and his tail curled across the floor, and thought about it for a long time, the way he did when he wanted to get something exactly right.

"He came up that drive like he owned you," he said finally. "Like he could stand at the bottom of your mum's steps and call you down off the porch, and you'd come." His ears went flat. "Okes like that—you fight them, you're telling them they matter. They *want* the fight. They know what to do with a fight. It's the thing they're good at. They've got a pack and a plan and somebody to blame afterward."

"So you didn't give him one."

"So I didn't give him one." Harry looked at his own forepaw, lying on the rug. "I wanted him to walk home thinking he'd spent twenty minutes talking to somebody who forgot his name before he got to the end of the drive." He paused. "And Layla was fifteen feet away through a wall. I wasn't going to start anything with her fifteen feet away. And I promised you about the peninsula."

"You put Paul in a puddle."

"Paul put Paul in a puddle. I just picked which one."

Leah laughed, and Layla made a cross noise at her for jiggling, and she stopped.

"And," said Harry.

He said it to the rug.

"And what?"

"And I wanted to take his head off." He said it in a perfectly ordinary voice. "I've seen your face when you say his name. Twice. Once in Mongolia. Once just now, when he said yours. I've seen what that face does." His claws flexed against the floorboards, once, and relaxed. "I was standing on that porch thinking about it the whole time. How fast. How far it would go."

Leah looked at him.

"Yeah?" she said.

"Ja."

"So why didn't you?"

"Because you'd have had to watch," said Harry. "And then you'd have had to live here. And your mum would have had to look at his wife in the Thriftway every week for the rest of her life." He shrugged his big shoulders. "And because it'd have made him the most important thing that happened tonight. He doesn't get to be that."

Leah didn't say anything for a while.

She looked down at Layla. At the tiny furred face pressed against her, eyes squeezed shut, the pale gray stripe down the middle of it. At the silver-tipped ears. She thought about Sam at the edge of the puddle, reaching for the hook in front of everybody and finding nothing.

"You said you'd explain," said Harry quietly. "Imprinting."

Leah closed her eyes.

"Okay," she said.

She told him.

She told him how it went. How a wolf saw somebody—just *saw* them, across a room, across a beach, the first time—and the whole world tilted. How everything else let go. How the person they'd seen became the only thing holding them to the ground. Like gravity, Jacob had thought once, in his head, back before it happened to him. Like steel cables. You'd be anything for them. Brother. Friend. Protector. Whatever they needed. And when they were old enough, if they wanted, more than that.

She told him what the elders said it was for. That it was the wolf choosing. Finding the best match. Making the line stronger, carrying the blood forward. She'd always thought that part through more than the others had, because the others had never needed to think about why the wolf would skip somebody.

She told him about the day Emily came to visit.

She'd been at Sam's mother's, in the kitchen. Sam had been gone two weeks, missing, and come back changed—burning hot, enormous, silent, refusing to tell her anything—and she'd been trying so hard to hold on. And Emily had come down from the Makah reservation for the weekend, to see her, to help, her cousin and her best friend. And Sam had walked into the kitchen, and looked at Emily. And Leah had watched his face.

"He didn't choose," she said. "That's the thing everyone always says. Like it makes it better. Sam didn't choose. The wolf did. It just happened to him." She looked down at Layla. "And then I phased. And I was in his head. And I got to feel it. Every day. What she felt like to him. What she smelled like, how he felt when she laughed. It was like being inside a love song about somebody else, with the volume all the way up, and no off switch."

Harry hadn't moved. His tail had gone completely still on the floor.

"So it's a compulsion," he said.

"They call it destiny."

"They would."

He said it very quietly. His voice had gone low and level, the way it had gone in the clearing above Tamarack Crossing, when the dead girls came up out of the ground.

"And Jacob—"

"Bella's baby," said Leah. "Seth said. The day she was born, probably. Knowing Jacob." She heard her own voice go thin. "Quil imprinted on a two-year-old. Emily's niece. Claire. He used to babysit her. He used to sit on the floor and play dolls with her while he—" She stopped.

Harry's claws had come out. All five on his right forepaw, slowly, and sunk about a quarter-inch into her mother's floorboards. He looked down at them like they belonged to somebody else, and drew them back in.

"Harry," said Leah. "There are nineteen of them now. Most of them haven't imprinted yet. Jacob wanted to see her. They're *all* going to want to see her. Every single one of those boys is going to want to look at her."

"Ja," said Harry.

"So what if—"

"Nobody's coming near her."

"We don't know how it works. We don't even know if it *can* happen to her. She's not—she's half you. She's mostly you. But she's part of this, too. She's from here." Her voice cracked. "I brought her here. I brought her *here*, Harry."

He got up off the floor. He came and sat down on the edge of the narrow bed next to her, and the whole bed groaned and tipped toward him, and he put one arm around her and the other forepaw very carefully on Layla's back.

"Then I'll find out how it works," he said.

"And if you can't stop it?"

He was quiet.

"Then we get back on the plane," he said.

He just said it, like it was the easiest thing in the world. *Then we get back on the plane.* That was the whole plan.

Leah put her head on his shoulder.

They sat there for a while. Layla finished eating and let go, and Leah lifted her up onto her shoulder and patted her back, and Layla let out a burp much too big for anything her size, and then sneezed, and then went limp and boneless with sleep.

"Can I tell you something?" Leah said.

"Always."

"When you asked me out. At the Bent Antler. Over the nachos." She picked at a thread on the star quilt. "I waited for it. I sat there and I waited for it to hit me. The pull. The tilt. I thought—maybe that's what this is. Maybe my wolf's finally done it to me. Maybe I'm about to stop being able to say no." She swallowed. "I sat there and waited for it to grab me by the gut."

"And?"

"Nothing came." She looked up at him. "Just me. And you. And a cold basket of wings. I *picked* you, Harry. Nobody made me."

His ears came up, very slowly.

"I know," he said.

"How do you know?"

"You took forty seconds to answer," said Harry. "You looked at the nachos the whole time. If it'd been magic, you'd have said yes before I finished the question." His tail had started moving again on the floor, just the tip of it. "Like on the motorbike."

"That was different. That was moving in."

"That was a bit magic," Harry admitted.

Leah laughed. Layla grumbled on her shoulder. And down the hall, her mother called, "Leah! Come get your husband out of my kitchen before he eats all the fry bread!"

They looked at each other.

"I'm right here," said Harry. "I'm in here."

"*Then come and help peel these potatoes!*"

Sue Clearwater had seen a great many things in her kitchen.

She'd seen her husband come in from the river at five in the morning with a forty-pound king salmon over his shoulder, grinning like a kid. She'd seen her son, at fifteen, come through the back wall of the house on four legs, in the middle of a fever, and stand in the wreckage of the mudroom shaking and whining while she held a dish towel to the cut on his muzzle and told him it was all right, baby, it was all right. She'd seen Sam Uley sit at this table with his head in his hands and tell her, very gently, that the stories were true. She'd seen Charlie Swan try to fix her garbage disposal with a butter knife.

She had not, until tonight, seen a sack of potatoes peel itself.

It happened about ninety seconds after she put the peeler in his paw. He'd come in when she called, ducking under the doorframe, and stood in the middle of her kitchen like a man in a china shop who'd been told not to touch anything, and she'd handed him the old peeler with the wooden handle and pointed at the sack on the counter. He'd looked at the peeler. It looked like a toothpick in his claws. He'd turned it over a couple of times. Then he'd looked at the sack.

The potatoes got up.

They rose out of the burlap one at a time and hung in a neat row in the air over the sink, a dozen of them, turning slowly. And then the skins began to come off, all at once, every potato at the same time, in long thin unbroken spirals, the way her mother used to peel apples at Christmas to show off. The peels dropped one after another into the compost bucket by the back door. The naked potatoes drifted down into the big pot of cold water on the stove and went under without a splash.

It took about eight seconds.

Harry set the peeler down on the counter, carefully, like he didn't want to hurt its feelings.

Sue looked at the pot. She looked at the compost bucket. She looked up at him.

"Huh," she said.

His ears went pink inside. "Sorry. Should've asked."

"Do the carrots," said Sue.

He did the carrots.

The steelhead was the next thing. Charlie had brought it by the week before, a beautiful bright hen he'd caught on the Bogachiel and cleaned himself, and she'd put it in the freezer meaning to have him over for it, and it was frozen solid as a brick. She'd set it in a sink of cold water when the car pulled up, out of pure habit, before she knew who was in it. It would be hours.

Harry looked at it.

Steam came off the fish, a thin gray curl, and the frost on its skin went to water and ran off into the sink. Sue reached in and pressed her thumb against the side of it. It gave under her thumb like a fish that had come out of the river that morning.

"That would've taken me all night," she said.

"Sorry."

"Stop saying sorry. Get me the big roasting pan. Bottom cupboard, left side."

He got her the big roasting pan. He had to kneel on her kitchen floor to reach the bottom cupboard, and his tail swept the whole length of the linoleum behind him and knocked the dog dish across the room, the one that had belonged to her husband's old Lab, Duke, dead six years, that she'd never been able to throw out. He picked it up and put it back exactly where it had been, and lined it up with the crack in the tile, the way it had been lined up.

Sue watched him do it and didn't say anything.

The phone rang.

She knew who it was before she picked it up. There was only one person who'd call at this hour on a Friday, and he'd have been wondering all afternoon.

"Sue," said Charlie's voice, in her ear. "You're not gonna believe this. There's a car—I'm on 101, I'm on my way home, and there was this car came through town around four, this huge black—"

"It's in my driveway," said Sue.

There was a pause on the line.

"It's *what*?"

Behind her, Sue heard Leah come into the kitchen with the baby. She heard her stop.

"Leah's home, Charlie," said Sue.

Another pause, longer.

"Leah," said Charlie. His voice changed. It went quiet and careful, the way it did when there was something big on the other end of a call. "Your Leah?"

"My Leah."

"Is she okay?"

"She's fine. She's—" Sue looked over at her daughter, standing in the kitchen doorway with Layla against her shoulder and her mouth hanging open. "She's married, Charlie. And she brought a baby. I'm a grandmother."

Charlie didn't say anything for so long she thought the line had dropped.

"Well," he said finally. "Well. Huh. Congratulations, Sue." He cleared his throat. "Do you want me to—I can come by. Or not. Whatever you want."

"Come tomorrow," said Sue. "Come for lunch. Tonight's family."

"Okay," said Charlie. "Okay. Tomorrow." Another pause. "Sue? The guy driving that car. He's not—he's not one of *them*, is he? The—" She could hear him not saying it. Charlie had been not saying it for a year. "Bella's lot?"

Sue looked across the kitchen at the six-foot-seven black wolf in her pantry doorway, who was holding a roasting pan in both forepaws and trying very hard to look like he wasn't listening.

"No," she said. "He's not one of them."

"Okay," said Charlie. "Good. That's—good. See you tomorrow."

She hung up.

Leah was staring at her.

"Why," said Leah, slowly, "is Charlie Swan calling you about cars?"

"Because he's a cop," said Sue, "and it's a car."

"*Mom*."

Sue went back to the steelhead. She took a lemon out of the bowl on the windowsill and started slicing it.

"Because I'm seeing him," she said.

The kitchen was very quiet.

"You're—" Leah said. "You're *seeing*—*Charlie*? Charlie *Swan*? *Bella's dad?*"

"He's a good man, Leah."

"He's *Bella's dad*!"

"He fixed the gutter," said Sue.

"The gutter's still *sagging*!"

"He tried," said Sue, with dignity.

Seth came in from the hall in a clean T-shirt, the old Forks Spartans one with the hole in the collar, with his hair still sticking up from the towel. He took one look at his sister's face and grinned.

"Oh, she told you," he said. "They're *so* cute. He brings her fish. He watches the Mariners with her and pretends he doesn't know she's reading through it. He said 'I love you' at the Fourth of July and then went and hid in the garage for twenty minutes—"

"*Seth*," said Sue.

"What? He did."

Leah sat down very slowly at the kitchen table with Layla on her shoulder.

"Harry," she said. "My mom is dating Charlie Swan."

"Ja, I heard," said Harry, from the pantry door. "He sounds nice."

"He's *Bella's dad*, Harry."

"He fixed the gutter," said Harry.

Sue turned around so neither of them would see her face.

---

She went into the pantry for the green beans and came out with the jar.

She hadn't meant to. She'd gone in for the green beans, the ones she'd put up in August, and her hand had gone past them to the back of the top shelf, to the place behind the flour where she kept it, and closed around the jar, and brought it out.

It was a quart Mason jar, the old kind with the wire bail. Inside, packed in tight, pink and gold and glistening, there was smoked salmon. On the lid was a strip of masking tape, and on the masking tape, in black marker, in the big square capitals of a man who'd never once in his life written anything in lowercase if he could help it:

*COHO — SEPT 05 — H.C.*

She put it down on the table.

Leah looked at it. Sue watched her daughter read the tape. Watched her face.

"Mom," said Leah.

"He put up thirty jars that fall," said Sue. "It was a good year. The coho ran big, and he and Seth went out every morning for three weeks. I gave most of it away. After." She touched the lid. "I kept the last one. I didn't know what I was keeping it for."

She looked across the table at the baby asleep on Leah's shoulder.

"I know now," she said.

Leah didn't say anything. She put her free hand over her mouth.

Harry had come out of the pantry doorway. He stood behind Leah's chair, stooped under the ceiling, and he looked at the jar on the table for a long moment, and at the tape, and at the handwriting. Then he looked at Sue. His ears had gone down, soft and low, the way she'd seen them go on the porch when he'd heard the name Harry come out of his own mouth in front of her.

He didn't say anything either.

Sue got the opener out of the drawer and broke the seal.

---

They ate at seven.

She set the table for four. She put out the good plates, her mother's, with the blue rims, and the cloth napkins she only used at Thanksgiving. Harry sat on the bench under the window again, with his knees up near his chin, and the bench groaned every time he moved, and he kept apologizing to it.

He was halfway through putting a plate together for himself when his ears went around toward the back of the house.

Both of them. All at once. Like a pair of satellite dishes.

"He's still out there," he said.

Sue looked at him. Then she got up and went to the window over the sink and looked out.

It was full dark now, and the rain had slackened to a drizzle. The light from the kitchen reached about halfway across the backyard, to the woodpile and the old shed with its tin roof. And beside the woodshed, lying in the wet grass with his chin on his paws and his ears pricked toward the house, there was a wolf.

He was young. She could tell from here. Long-legged and narrow through the chest, not filled out yet, the way Seth had looked the first year. Brown, a muddy middling brown, except for one ear, the left one, which was pale, almost white, like somebody had dipped it in cream.

"That's Brady Fuller," said Sue. "His mother works the front desk at the clinic in Forks. He's fifteen."

"He's been out there two hours," said Harry.

"Sam put him there."

"Ja." Harry looked down at the plate in front of him. He'd been putting fry bread on it. "Is he allowed to eat? On duty?"

Sue thought about that. "He's fifteen," she said. "He's always allowed to eat."

Harry got up off the bench. He took a second plate off the stack on the counter—not her mother's, one of the old chipped everyday ones—and put three pieces of fry bread on it, and a big slab of the steelhead, and a pile of potatoes, and then, after a second's thought, a fourth piece of fry bread. Then he went to the back door.

The screen door was still hanging off its top hinge where Seth had come through it. Harry looked at it on his way past. The hinge screwed itself back into the frame with a small, squeaky, determined sound, and the screen door swung upright and settled into place and shut itself with a neat little *click*.

Harry opened it again. He held the plate out over the back step. And the plate floated off his paw and went out into the dark, across the wet grass, slow and level, about two feet off the ground, like a waiter crossing a dining room. It set down in the grass in front of the woodshed, about three feet from the brown wolf's nose.

The wolf's head came up off his paws.

He looked at the plate. He looked at the house. He looked at the plate again. His pale ear swiveled around, and then the brown one.

Harry shut the screen door and came back and sat down on the bench and picked up his fork.

Sue stayed at the window a little longer. She watched Brady Fuller get up, slowly, and stretch his long legs out in front of him, and walk over to the plate, and sniff it. He looked at the house one more time. Then he put his head down and ate the whole thing in about forty-five seconds, fish and potatoes and all four pieces of fry bread, and licked the plate until it shone, and lay back down beside it with his chin on the rim.

Sue came back to the table and sat down.

Nobody said anything about it. Seth was looking at Harry with his mouth open. Leah wasn't looking at anybody; she was cutting her steelhead with a little smile on her face.

Harry ate.

Leah had told her about this on the porch, in the middle of everything else—*he eats, Mom, you're not gonna believe how much he eats*—and Sue had been married to a fisherman for twenty-two years and raised two wolves, and she'd thought she knew what that meant. She counted. She couldn't help it. Seven pieces of fry bread. Half the steelhead, which had been a nine-pound fish. Most of the potatoes. Most of the carrots. A third of the jar of smoked salmon, which he ate very slowly, one piece at a time, with his eyes half closed. At one point Seth and Harry both reached for the last piece of fry bread on the plate at the same moment, and looked at each other, and Harry let go, and Seth stared at him like he'd been given a car.

"So," said Sue.

Harry looked up.

"Tell me about yourself," she said. "Leah said a little. On the porch. Not enough."

He put down his fork. He wiped his muzzle with her Thanksgiving napkin. His ears went sideways, and he looked at Leah, and Leah shrugged at him.

"What do you want to know?" he said.

"Start with your mother," said Sue.

His ears went still.

"Lily," he said, after a moment. "Her name was Lily. She was a witch. Her parents weren't—they were ordinary. Muggles. That's our word for it. My dad was James. They were twenty-one." He looked at his plate. "There was a man. A wizard. He was trying to take over, and there was a war about it, and he came to the house on Halloween when I was one. He killed my dad at the bottom of the stairs. Then he killed my mum in front of my cot. Then he tried to kill me, and it didn't work."

He said it all very plainly. Like he was reading it off a card.

Seth had stopped eating.

"I went to my mum's sister," said Harry. "My aunt. She and her husband had a boy my age. They didn't want me. I slept in the cupboard under the stairs till I was eleven." He turned his fork over on the tablecloth. "Then I got a letter. Wizard school. I had friends there. Ron and Hermione. And then the man who killed my parents came back, and there was another war, and I was in it. I won it when I was seventeen."

"Seventeen," said Sue.

"Ja."

Sue looked at her son, sixteen, sitting across the table in his old Spartans T-shirt with a piece of fry bread forgotten in his hand. Sixteen, and he'd been running patrols for a year and a half. Sixteen, and he'd stood in a field in the snow last January with fifty vampires.

She didn't say anything about it. She reached over and took the fry bread out of Seth's hand, and put it back on his plate, and patted his hand, and Seth looked at her like she was crazy.

"And then I was a hit wizard," said Harry. "That's like a policeman. For wizards. I worked in South Africa for a long time, and then all over. I got bitten by a werewolf in Romania in '03. It went a bit strange." He gestured at himself, the fur, the ears. "And then I bought a house on a lake in Canada because it was quiet, and I was sitting in a diner in Tamarack Crossing eating pie, and Leah walked in and ordered six eggs and sat with her back to the wall."

"*Six eggs*," said Seth.

"Ja. And hash browns. And pancakes."

"That's not even *that* much," said Leah.

"I was very impressed," said Harry.

Sue looked at him for a long time. At the big black wolf on her bench with her mother's napkin in his paw. She thought about a cupboard under a staircase. She thought about a little boy sleeping in it.

"Thank you for telling me," she said.

His ears came up, a little.

"Can I ask you something?" he said. "About him."

He didn't say who. He didn't have to. He was looking at the jar on the table. *COHO — SEPT 05 — H.C.*

Sue felt her throat close up. She waited for it to open again.

"Ask," she said.

"What was he like?"

Nobody had asked her that. Not once, in twenty-one months. Everybody knew what he was like. Everybody had known him. They told her stories about him at the funeral, at the Thriftway, at the post office, until she wanted to scream. But nobody had sat at her table and asked her, because nobody needed to.

"He was loud," said Sue.

Leah laughed, a short, startled sound.

"He was so loud," said Sue. "He sang in the truck. He sang badly. He sang in the shower and in the boat and at the sink. He told the worst jokes you ever heard in your life. Knock-knock jokes. To grown men. At council meetings." She found she was smiling. "He fished with Charlie Swan every Saturday for twenty years and they never said more than fifteen words to each other the whole day and came home and said they'd had a great talk. He played cribbage with Billy Black on Tuesdays and cheated. He snuck cigarettes on the back step after dinner and thought I didn't know."

"You knew?" said Leah.

"Of course I knew. I could smell it on him. I let him have it." Sue looked down at the table. "He taught you to gut a salmon on the tailgate of the truck when you were seven. You cried because the fish looked at you. He said, *There you go, kiddo, now you'll never starve.* And then he took you for ice cream and made you promise not to tell me you'd cried."

Leah had both hands over her mouth now.

"Do you have a picture?" said Harry quietly.

Sue got up.

She went down the hall to her room and came back with the frame off the nightstand. The one from Ruby Beach. She put it on the table in front of Harry, and he picked it up in both forepaws, very carefully, and looked at it.

It was the four of them on a gray day, on the gray sand, with the sea stacks behind them and the wind blowing everybody's hair sideways. Harry Clearwater in the middle, big and broad and grinning so wide his eyes had nearly disappeared, with one arm locked around Sue's shoulders like he was afraid she might blow out to sea. Seth at seven, missing both front teeth, holding up a crab. And Leah at eleven, at the end, in a Seahawks T-shirt three sizes too big for her, with her arms folded and her hair in her mouth and a scowl on her face that could have stripped paint.

Harry looked at it for a long time.

Then he looked up at Leah. Then back down at the picture. Then up at Leah again.

"You haven't changed," he said.

Leah threw a piece of fry bread at his head.

He caught it without looking. He caught it in his *tail*, which came up over his shoulder from behind the bench and plucked it out of the air like a hand, and brought it around, and dropped it neatly onto his plate.

And Sue laughed.

She laughed out loud at her own kitchen table, with her husband's picture in front of her and his last jar of salmon open on the cloth. She laughed until she had to put her napkin over her face. She couldn't remember the last time. She tried, and she couldn't. Somewhere in there, under the napkin, she noticed she was crying too, and she didn't care. Seth was laughing. Leah was laughing, with her hands over her face, shaking her head. The baby woke up on Leah's shoulder and made a small outraged noise about all of it and sneezed.

Harry sat on the bench with her husband's picture in his paws and his ears all the way up, and his tail, under the table, was going *thump, thump, thump* against her kitchen floor.

---

Seth had about nine hundred questions, and he'd been sitting on all of them since the porch.

He'd been good. He'd put on a shirt. He'd peeled nothing, because the potatoes had peeled themselves. He'd eaten his dinner and not asked anything while his mom talked about Dad, because his mom hadn't talked about Dad like that in almost two years, and Seth had sat there with his fork in his hand and listened to every word and not wanted it to stop.

But the laughing was dying down now. His mom had put her napkin back in her lap. Harry was still on the bench under the window, still holding the Ruby Beach picture in both forepaws, looking down at it with his ears up. Leah had Layla on her chest and was jiggling her. And Seth couldn't stand it anymore.

"So you got bit," he said. "By a *werewolf*. Like a real one."

Harry looked up from the photo.

"Ja," he said.

"And it turned you into—" Seth waved a hand at all of him. "That."

"Sort of." Harry set the picture down on the table, carefully, facing Sue. "Werewolves are a curse. Once a month, full moon, you turn into a monster and you don't know who you are, and you'd kill your own mum. That's what was supposed to happen." He turned his fork over. "Didn't. I went into a cell in Geneva at the full moon and came out the next morning like this, with my own head still on. The Healers took a lot of blood off me after that. A year of it. In the end their best guess was there'd been something in my blood already. Something old. Asleep. And the bite woke it up, and it ate the curse."

"Something like what?" said Seth.

"Wolf," said Harry. "That's all they could tell me. Old, and wolf, and nothing to do with any curse."

Across the table, Seth's mom had gone still.

Seth noticed it because his mom never went still. She was always doing something. Cutting something, wiping something, folding something. Now she was sitting with her hands flat on either side of her plate, looking at Harry.

"Where from?" she said. "Did they say where it came from?"

"My dad's side, they thought. Through the Blacks." Harry's ears flicked. "One of the Healers found a note in an old family book. Some Black, a couple of hundred years back. He'd sailed off to the Americas on a fur company ship, and come home years later with—" he paused, remembering it "—*more than furs*. That's all it said. I thought it was interesting for about a week. Then I got sent to Chechnya."

"Harry," said Leah. She'd stopped jiggling the baby. "You never told me that."

"Never thought about it. It was one line in a book." He looked at her, and then at Sue, and his head tilted. "Why?"

Seth's mom didn't answer right away. She picked up her water glass and drank some and put it down again.

"When I was a girl," she said, "Old Quil used to tell a story at the bonfires. Not often. Maybe once every few years. He didn't like telling it, because it was a sad one, and a small one. Just a story about a family." She turned the glass around on the tablecloth. "In the time of the fur ships. When the ships came up the coast to trade. One of them got caught in a storm in the fall and came into the river mouth with a broken mast, and had to stay the winter. And there was a man on it. A pale man. And he wasn't like the others on the ship."

Seth had never heard this. He looked at Leah. Leah was looking at their mom.

"He could make fire without flint," Sue said. "Animals followed him. The dogs in the village wouldn't bark at him. And there was a woman. From one of the old families. The families that had the wolves in them." She was quiet a moment. "They loved each other. That's how Old Quil told it. And in the spring, when the ship was mended, she went with him. Across the sea. Her father said she was dead to them, and her family stopped saying her name. And her brothers went into the forest and ran as wolves for three days, and howled the whole time."

The kitchen was very quiet. The rain had stopped on the roof. Seth could hear the refrigerator humming.

"What family?" said Harry. His voice had gone low.

"I don't know," said Sue. "He never said. I'd have to ask." She looked at Leah. "Billy might know. Old Quil would."

Leah looked back at her. Something passed between them that Seth didn't get, at first.

And then he did get it, all at once, like falling through ice, and he sat there with his mouth open.

*Potter-Black.* On the porch. *Like—Black? Like Jake?*

Nobody said it. His mom didn't say it, and Leah didn't say it, and Harry definitely didn't say it, though Seth could see in the way his ears had gone very still and very straight that he was thinking something. Seth opened his mouth to say it.

Leah looked at him.

Seth closed his mouth.

"Huh," said Harry, finally, very quietly, to nobody.

He sat there a moment. Then he shook his head, a little, like a dog shaking off rain, and looked at Seth.

"Your turn," he said. "What's north of the lake?"

Seth swallowed.

He'd been thinking about it all through dinner, in the back of his head, every time Harry's ears turned toward the window. He'd been thinking about it since the porch, since Harry had stood there in that voice and said *rot and copper, under something sweet*, and Seth had felt every hair on his arms stand straight up.

"Okay," he said. "So you know there's something. Since October. We can't catch it. The trail just stops." He put his fork down. "But there's one more thing. Jake told us not to tell anybody outside the packs. Not even Mom."

His mom's eyebrows went up. Seth didn't look at her.

"November ninth," he said. "Embry was on the north shore. By the old railroad trail, the one that goes around the lake past the tunnels. He picked up the smell, really fresh, fresher than any of us had ever got it, and he went after it, and it was going really good, and he was really close. And then it stopped at this big rock by the water. Just stopped."

He could see it. That was the thing about the pack. He'd been there. He'd been phased, up by the Sol Duc, and he'd been in Embry's head when it happened, and he could still see it the way he'd seen it then, through Embry's eyes. The big gray boulder with moss on top. The black water. The trail running right up to the rock and ending, like somebody had cut it off with scissors.

"And then there was this sound," Seth said. "Embry couldn't tell where from. It was like it was coming from everywhere. From the trees. From the water. From inside his own ears." He looked down at the table. "Somebody humming."

Across the table, Leah's fork hit her plate.

Seth looked up. His sister had gone gray, like the color had run out of her face and down into her neck. Her hand had gone to the baby on her chest and stopped there, flat.

Harry hadn't moved. He hadn't moved at all. But his tail, which had been going against the floor all through dinner, every time anybody laughed, had stopped dead.

"What happened then," said Harry.

"Embry doesn't know. He blacked out. He woke up an hour later in the ferns about fifty yards back from the rock, phased back, and he had a cut on his shoulder. Right here." Seth touched the top of his own left shoulder, by the collarbone. "Like four inches long. Really neat. Like somebody did it with a scalpel. And it took two days to heal." He looked at Harry. "We heal in, like, an hour. Two days. Jake made him go see Carlisle."

"Did Carlisle say anything?" said Leah. Her voice sounded strange.

"He said it was clean. He said whoever did it knew what they were doing." Seth hesitated. "He said it looked like a sample."

Nobody said anything.

"Seth," said Harry. "The humming. Did Embry remember it? The tune?"

"I remember it," said Seth. "I was in his head. It was really short. It kept going around." He frowned, trying to get it right. Then he hummed it.

Three notes. Up, then down. Then nothing. Like a question nobody answered.

Leah made a sound. Just a small one, in her throat.

Harry sat on the bench under the window, perfectly still, with his ears pinned so flat to his skull they'd almost disappeared into the fur. His eyes had gone to the window. To the dark, and the trees, and somewhere a long way past them, north.

"Vulk's dead," Leah said. To Harry, not to anybody else. "Harry. I *watched*. You burned him to nothing. You crushed the jar."

"Ja," said Harry.

"Then who's *humming*?"

Harry didn't answer her. He put both forepaws flat on the table and pushed himself up off the bench.

"Sit down," said Sue.

He stopped, halfway up, and looked at her.

"Sue—"

"Sit down," said Seth's mom again. She hadn't raised her voice. She'd used the one she used on Seth when he was eight and trying to sneak out after dinner to go down to the beach. "Whatever it is, it's been out there since October. It'll still be out there after pie."

Harry stayed where he was for a long second, half standing, his head nearly touching the kitchen ceiling.

Then he sat back down. The bench groaned.

"Is there pie?" he said.

"There's no pie," said Sue. "I didn't know you were coming. There's ice cream."

Harry looked at her. Then he reached into the inside pocket of his coat, and felt around for a second, and brought out something about the size of a coaster, wrapped in a tea towel. He put it on the table and unwrapped it. It was a pie. A tiny pie, perfect, with a lattice crust and something dark purple bubbling up through the gaps. As Seth watched, it grew. It got bigger and bigger, smoothly, the way the car had in the drive, until it was sitting on his mom's tablecloth in a full-sized tin, still hot, smelling like sugar and berries and something spicy that Seth didn't know.

"Kreacher packed it," said Harry. "He packs pie the way other people pack a first-aid kit."

"Bumbleberry," said Leah faintly.

"Darlene's recipe."

Seth didn't know who Darlene was, or Kreacher, or why his sister was suddenly laughing in a high, wobbly way with her hand over her eyes. But he knew it was pie. He got the knife.

---

Layla woke Leah at ten past two, the way she always did, with a thin rising whimper that turned into a yell if you let it.

Leah sat up in the dark. For a second she didn't know where she was. Then she did. Her own room. Her own window. Her own star quilt, except the bed under it was about four times wider than it had been when she went to sleep, because Harry had looked at the twin bed at eleven o'clock and then looked at himself, and the bed had grown until it touched both walls and the end of it was pressed against the closet door, and you had to climb in from the hallway. The Huskies pennant hung over the headboard at a slightly different angle. She'd decided not to care.

The other side of the bed was empty.

Layla was on Leah's chest, where Harry had put her when he got up. Leah fed her in the dark, and listened to the house. The refrigerator. Her mom's breathing through the wall, slow and deep. Seth snoring on the couch, because he'd refused to go to his own room in case he missed something. And outside, nothing. The rain had stopped.

When Layla was done, Leah wrapped her up in Kreacher's blanket and climbed out over the end of the bed and went down the hall.

The front-room curtain rod was back on the wall. She noticed it on her way past. The curtain hung straight, the way it had before her mom ripped it down at the sight of the car.

She opened the door and went out onto the porch.

The sky had cleared. It was cold, properly cold, and still, and there were stars over the alders, more than she remembered there ever being over La Push. The Christmas lights were still on along the rail. And Harry was standing at the edge of the yard by the biggest alder, with one forepaw flat against its trunk and his tail hanging still, and the air around his paw was shimmering like heat coming off a road in August.

She stood on the top step and watched him.

He took his paw off the tree. The shimmer faded. He turned his head and saw her and his ears came up.

"You should be asleep," he said.

"She was hungry." Leah came down the steps. She stopped. Then she looked down at her own feet.

The puddle was gone.

The low spot at the bottom of the steps, the one that had been there every winter of her life, was full. Level, smooth, packed tight with clean gray gravel that matched the rest of the drive. She stood on it in her socks.

"You filled in Dad's puddle," she said.

Harry's ears went back. "Should I put it back?"

Leah thought about it. She thought about her dad standing on this porch every November saying *I'm gonna get to that, Sue, this weekend*, and never getting to it, for twenty years.

"No," she said. "He'd have liked that somebody finally did it."

She looked up at the corner of the house. The gutter on the left side ran straight and level along the edge of the roof, the way it hadn't since she was in middle school.

"And the gutter."

"Don't tell Charlie," said Harry.

She came over and stood next to him under the alder. He put an arm around her and the baby both, and she leaned into him. His coat was cold and damp on the outside from the night air and hot underneath.

"How many?" she said.

"Three," said Harry.

She looked at him.

"And a small one," Harry admitted.

"What does the small one do?"

"Anybody comes over that line meaning harm to this house," said Harry, "ends up back wherever they started from." His ears flicked. "As a ferret."

"Harry."

"It's a very good ward. Hermione did the maths."

Behind the woodshed, something snored. Leah looked over. Brady Fuller was still there, a long brown shape curled up nose to tail in the grass beside the empty plate, with his pale ear twitching in his sleep. His fur looked dry.

"The rain was getting in his ears," said Harry. "He went to sleep about an hour ago. I don't think Sam's going to be very happy with him."

Leah looked north.

There was nothing to see that way. Alders, and the dark shoulder of the hill, and above that the stars. Fourteen miles of trees between here and 101, and past 101, the long black water of the lake. And somewhere out there on the north shore, a big gray rock with moss on top where a trail stopped.

Three notes. Up, and down, and stop.

"She's one of us," Leah said quietly. "Layla. She's born to it, Harry. Two kinds of it. And you heard what he said on the ridge. What our hearts are worth to something like that."

"I know," said Harry.

"So should we go? Should we get back on the plane?"

He was quiet for a long time.

"If we go, it's still here," he said finally. "So's your mum. So's Seth. So's that kid." He nodded toward the woodshed. "And Dylan Littlesea, who's thirteen and went through the wall of his classroom. It's been here two months. It's been watching. It took a sample off Embry in November." His forepaw tightened, very slightly, on her shoulder. "It knows this coast better than I do."

"And if we stay?"

"Then in the morning I go and smell that rock."

Leah looked up at him.

"I'm coming," she said.

His ears tipped toward her.

"Is that you asking?" he said.

"That's me telling."

He thought about that. Then his ears came all the way up, and the corner of his muzzle went up with them, off a lot of teeth.

"Fair," said Harry.

They stood there under the alder in the cold with the stars out, her and him and the baby asleep between them. After a while Layla stirred in the blanket and made a small sleepy grumble, and Harry bent his head and licked her ears, one and then the other, slow, and she started to purr.

His tail started going against the trunk of the alder. *Thump. Thump. Thump.*

Behind the woodshed, Brady Fuller lifted his head at the sound, and looked at them for a long moment with his pale ear pricked. Then he put his chin back down on the plate and went back to sleep.
